Hockey is fast. Nobody has time to think about a tactics board during a game. That is why
one simple question helps: **Which role do I have right now?**

There are only four. Which one you have depends on two questions: does our team have the
puck? And do you have the puck yourself – or does your opponent?

The roles come from the training framework of the German Ice Hockey Federation (DEB). For
U13 it describes attacking with and without the puck in the chapter
<a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/grundlagentraining/zweikampf-offensiv">Zweikampf offensiv</a>
and defending in the chapter
<a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/grundlagentraining/zweikampf-defensiv">Zweikampf defensiv</a>
(both in German). The roles are often called O1, O2, D1 and D2 for short. O stands for
offence, D for defence. Our team gives the roles names, and we use the German names on the
ice too: **König** (king), **Satellit** (satellite), **Jäger**
(hunter) and **Wächter** (guard).

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
**We have the puck:**
- **König** (O1) – you have the puck.
- **Satellit** (O2) – a teammate has the puck.

**The opponents have the puck:**
- **Jäger** (D1) – you defend against the opponent with the puck.
- **Wächter** (D2) – you defend against an opponent without the puck.

The role does not depend on the position. A defender can be König, a forward can be
Wächter.
</Callout>

## Roles change all the time

The roles are not positions like centre or defender. They change with every pass and every
lost puck.

- The König passes. Now the same player is a Satellit, and the teammate with the puck is König.
- The opponents take the puck from us. Now everyone is a Jäger or a Wächter. Whoever is
  closest to the puck becomes the Jäger.
- We win the puck back. Whoever has it is König. Everyone else is a Satellit straight away.

## König: the player with the puck

The **König** has the puck and decides what happens next. That is a responsibility: a good
König doesn't just get rid of the puck, but looks, skates and then plays a solution. This
fits our <a href={href('/en/season-concept')}>season concept</a>: we never clear the puck
blindly.

**Head up, scan the ice.** Scanning means a quick look across the ice. Where are your
teammates, where are the opponents, where is there space? It starts before the puck
arrives. Anyone who only looks once they have the puck is often too late.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="scan" locale="en" />

**Find open ice.** Don't skate into traffic, the places where many players are. Speed and
quick changes of direction get you away from your opponent. And look for the way to the
goal: the best shots come from the slot, right in front of the goal. Why open ice is worth
so much is explained in the <a href={href('/en/time-and-space')}>time and space</a> article.

<RinkPlay play="open-ice" locale="en" />

**Protect the puck.** Every opponent wants to get a stick on the puck. So don't hold the
puck out in front of you like on a tray. Carry it on the side away from the opponent. Your
body is then between the puck and the opponent.

<PuckControl variant="shield" />

**Puck on the forehand.** The **forehand** is the side of the blade you normally shoot
with. With the puck there, you have three options: pass, shoot or keep skating. The
opponent doesn't know what is coming.

<PuckControl variant="forehand" />

**Dekes in a 1‑on‑1.** A **deke** is a fake. You point one way with your stick, body or
head and skate the other way. That pulls the opponent out of position.

## Satellit: attackers without the puck

A **satellite** circles a planet. In hockey the Satelliten circle the König. In attack,
four out of five players are Satelliten. Their job: the König is never alone. Make
yourself available for a pass!

**Get out of the passing shadow.** The **passing shadow** is the space behind an opponent.
No pass gets through to it. If you can't see the König's blade, you are in the shadow. One
or two steps to the side and you are open.

**Don't hide.** Standing behind an opponent or far from the play doesn't help the König.
Skate to where the König can see you: in front, beside or behind.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="pass-shadow" locale="en" />

**Turn your head.** Look around, even without the puck. Knowing where the opponents are
helps you find the gap faster.

**Stick on the ice, in the open passing lane.** That shows the König where the pass should
go. And **talk**: a short call helps the König more than any hand signal.

**Skate towards the pass.** Then it is shorter and safer. More on this in the article
<a href={href('/en/skating-to-the-puck')}>Skating to the puck and crossing</a>.

**Spread out.** One near, one wide, one deep: the <a href={href('/en/support')}>Support</a>
article shows how the Satelliten spread out.

**Drive to the net.** Skating away from the König towards the goal often pulls an opponent
along. That opens space for the König. In front of the goal you block the goalie's view.
And if the puck bounces off, you are there first.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="net-drive" locale="en" />

<Callout type="tipp" title="Always ready">
A loose puck belongs to whoever gets there first. A Satellit keeps their legs moving, even
without the puck. Then the Satellit gets there faster.
</Callout>

## Jäger: defending the puck carrier

The **Jäger** defends against the opponent with the puck. Usually that is the player
closest to the puck. Four things make a good Jäger:

1. **Stay on the defensive side.** You are between the opponent and our goal. More on this
   in the <a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a> article.
2. **Close the gap quickly.** The **gap** is the distance between you and the opponent. The
   bigger it is, the more time and space your opponent has. A few quick strides forward – about
   five as a rule of thumb – take both away. In the drawing that is the purple circle; more
   in the <a href={href('/en/time-and-space')}>time and space</a> article.
3. **Stick on puck.** Your blade is on the ice, pointing at the puck. That puts on pressure
   without you leaning in too far.
4. **Push them outside.** Keep the opponent on the boards, away from the middle. The middle
   in front of our goal is the most dangerous place on the ice. Some call it *home base*.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="bubble-shrink" locale="en" />

### Where is the opponent looking?

A good Jäger reads the situation before attacking.

**The opponent is facing the play** and can see you, their teammates and the goal. Don't be
too wild. Close the gap quickly, get your stick on the puck and put on pressure. But stay
on the defensive side. Rushing in now gets you skated around.

**The opponent is facing the boards**, back to you, and can't see what is
happening. Now you can attack more firmly and separate them from the puck. Body contact is
part of it, for example pinning them on the boards as in the
<a href={href('/en/support')}>Support</a> article. A body check is not: it is banned up to
and including U13 (see <a href={href('/en/foundation-training')}>DEB foundation training</a>).
Here too: stick on puck, stay on the defensive side.

## Wächter: defending players without the puck

The **Wächter** defend against the opponents without the puck. These opponents are the
other team's Satelliten. They wait for a pass, often in front of our goal. The Wächter
makes sure the pass doesn't arrive – or that the opponent can't do anything with it.

**Stay on the defensive side.** Without the puck too, you stand between your opponent and
our goal.

**Opponent and puck in view.** Stand so that you can see both. If you can't, keep turning
your head from one to the other. Watching only the puck means losing your opponent.

**Stick on the ice.** Your blade is in the passing lane, where a pass to your opponent
would have to go. That way you can intercept passes.

**Work from the inside out.** First protect the middle in front of the goal. Only then go
outside.

**The closer to the goal, the tighter.** If your opponent is far from the goal, you can
leave some space. If they skate to the front of the goal, stay right with them.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="guard-tight" locale="en" />

**Talk.** Tell or show your teammates which opponent you are taking. Otherwise two of you
cover the same player and another one is left open. The
<a href={href('/en/coverage')}>coverage</a> article explains who takes which area.

<Callout type="tipp" title="Talking is training">
Calling out who you cover feels strange at first. It only becomes a habit if you do it at
every practice – not just in games.
</Callout>

## Switch fast

The most important moment is the switch. We lose the puck, and König and Satelliten become
Jäger and Wächter. Or we win the puck, and Jäger and Wächter become König and Satelliten.
This switch is called **transition**.

Whoever switches faster has more players in the right place for a moment. Many goals come
from that. That is why switching is worth practising: every time the puck is lost, ask
yourself straight away which role you have now.

**Watching teaches too.** In practice we often play on a small area, with a break between
shifts. Use it: pick a player and ask yourself which role they have right now. What do they do
well? What would you do differently?

**The roles as an operating system.** A computer needs an operating system so that
everything else can run. The four roles are something like that for hockey. If you keep
them in mind in every drill, you will do the right thing automatically more and more often.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents in the stands">
At the next game, pick one child. Every time the puck changes hands, ask yourself: is the
child König, Satellit, Jäger or Wächter right now? You will see how often the role changes
in a single minute.
</Callout>

## What to tell yourself

In a game there is no time to go through an article in your head. A short phrase always
fits, though. It is called a **cue** (Gedankenstütze): you say it to yourself, and it reminds
you of the most important thing in your role. [Studies in sport psychology](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691611413136)
show that short instructions to yourself help, especially with things you are just learning.
And they help more when you practise them.

It all starts with the question at the switch. Every time the puck changes teams:

**"Who's got the puck? Who am I?"**

Then comes the phrase for your role:

| Role | Tell yourself | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| König | **Look, skate, play** | First look, then skate into open ice, then play a solution. Never blind. |
| Satellit | **Out of the shadow** | Can't see the König's blade? One or two steps to the side. |
| Jäger | **Read first, then hunt** | Opponent facing the play: come in calmly. Back to you: attack. |
| Wächter | **Turn your head, see both** | Puck and opponent in view, not just the puck. |

When two are at the puck, only one attacks. That Jäger thinks: "I press, you help." The
helper thinks: "Stay close, eyes on the open one."

How it works:

- **One phrase per role.** Not five. If another phrase helps you more, use your own.
- **Out loud in training, in your head in games.** Say the phrase half aloud during drills.
  In a game it then comes by itself.
- **Say what you want to do.** "Turn your head" helps more than "don't just watch the puck".
