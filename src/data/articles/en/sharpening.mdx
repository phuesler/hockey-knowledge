"Those need sharpening again." You hear that sentence by the boards all the time. This
article explains what actually happens and why the number you give the sharpener makes such
a big difference.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
A blade doesn't glide on a flat surface but on **two edges**. Sharpening (Schleifen) grinds
a groove between those edges – the **hollow** (Hohlschliff). The deeper the groove, the more
grip and the less speed. For light U13 players the shop standard is often **too deep**.
</Callout>

## What happens when skates are sharpened

Look at a blade from underneath: it isn't flat. A groove runs down the middle, and two sharp
edges stand either side of it. These edges cut into the ice – they're the reason you can
turn and stop at all.

When sharpening, the blade runs over a grinding wheel that has been dressed to exactly that
curve. The machine removes material until the groove is clean again and both edges are the
same height.

The curve is given as a **radius**, usually in inches: 1/2", 5/8", 3/4". It's called the
**hollow** or *ROH* (radius of hollow).

## The radius: grip versus glide

Here's the one rule you need to remember:

> **Small radius = deep groove = lots of grip, little glide.**
> **Large radius = shallow groove = little grip, lots of glide.**

That seems back to front at first, but it's logical: a small circle is more strongly
curved, so the groove gets deeper and the edges stick out more sharply.

The groove is surprisingly shallow – at 1/2" it's less than a tenth of a millimetre. The

## Which hollow for U13?

Most shops sharpen to **1/2"** without asking, some to **5/8"**. That's a usable starting
point.

What really matters is **weight**. How hard an edge cuts into the ice depends on how much
weight presses on it. A 90-kilo player presses the edges deep into the ice; a 35-kilo child
doesn't come close. So:

**Try a shallower hollow**

Many U13 players skate much better on **5/8"** than on 1/2" – they glide more easily and
hardly lose any grip.

<Callout type="tipp" title="How to try it">
Only ever change **one step** at a time, so from 1/2" to 5/8", not straight to 3/4". Let your
child skate on it for two or three practices and then ask specifically: do you slide away
when you stop? Do you have to work harder on your starts? Write down the radius that worked
best – and ask for it every time from then on.
</Callout>

## Flat bottom V: the hollow that looks like a trapezoid

Besides the round hollow there are grinds with a **flat bottom**. The best known is called
**flat bottom V**, or **FBV** for short. The groove has a flat bottom and slanted sides like
a V. From the front it looks like a trapezoid.

The name depends on the sharpening machine. On Sparx machines there is the **FIRE** grinding
ring for it. There the sides are round like a normal hollow, only the bottom is flattened.
The idea is the same for all variants.

<GrindProfiles />

**The idea behind it.** The edges grip much like a deeper hollow. But because the bottom is
flat, the blade sinks into the ice less and glides more easily. Sparx says of its grind: a
1/2" FIRE grind has the grip of 1/2" and the glide of 11/16".

**What this means for U13.** Light kids press the edges only slightly into the ice. For them
a flat bottom can be pleasant: grip when stopping, and still easy skating. It's not a must.
A shallower round hollow like 5/8" goes in a similar direction – and every sharpener can do
that.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Before you switch">
- **Not every sharpener can do it.** It needs its own grinding wheel or ring.
- **The sizes have different names** from the hollow. Tell the sharpener your current radius
  and ask which setting matches it.
- **Don't switch back and forth.** Every switch between a round and a flat bottom costs more
  steel than a normal resharpening. So stick with the same grind and ideally the same
  sharpener.
- Here too: only change **one step** at a time and test it for two or three practices.
</Callout>

## How often to sharpen?

Don't count in weeks, count in **hours on the ice**. A good starting value is **15 to 20
hours on the ice**. With three practices a week, that's about every two to three weeks.

But that's only a guide value. Edges go dull faster because of:

- **Hard, cold ice** (rinks in winter, fresh after the ice resurfacer)
- **Bad habits**, above all a few steps on concrete or tiles
- **Blade guards packed away wet** – then the steel rusts

## Checking the edges yourself

You don't have to be a pro for this. Three tests that also work by the boards:

**The fingernail test.** Draw a fingernail across the edge – not along it! If it's sharp, it
shaves a fine trace off the nail. If the nail just slides over it, the edge is round. Check
both edges of each blade; they can be worn differently.

**Feel and look.** Carefully run your thumb along the edge. You feel nicks and dents
straight away. Also hold the blade up to the light: a sharp edge doesn't show a shiny strip,
a round one does.

**Ask your child.** That's the most reliable test. If they slide away when stopping or in
tight turns, the edges are due.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Nicks don't go away on their own">
A deep nick – typically from a step on concrete or from one blade knocking against another –
can't be fixed by hand. It has to be ground out, and that takes off more steel than a normal
sharpening.
</Callout>

## Looking after blades

This is where most of the damage is done – not on the ice.

- **Never walk on hard floors.** Concrete, tiles, metal grates: a single step ruins a fresh
  sharpening. In the dressing room and on the way to the ice, **hard plastic skate guards**
  (Kufenschoner) belong on the skates.
- **Take them off for storage.** In the bag, use **fabric soakers**. Hard plastic traps
  moisture, and the steel rusts overnight.
- **Always wipe them dry.** After every practice, wipe the blades with a cloth before the
  soakers go on. That takes ten seconds and prevents rust spots that would have to be ground
  out later.
- **Let skates dry in the open.** Don't forget them in the closed bag in the car.

<Callout type="achtung" title="New skates are usually not ready to play">
Skates usually come out of the box **unsharpened**. They look sharp, but they have no hollow.
The first trip after buying them is to the sharpener – otherwise your child just slides
around at the first practice and gets frustrated.
</Callout>

## At the sharpener

What you say: **"5/8, please"** – in a German shop, **„Bitte auf 5/8"** – or whichever radius
you've written down. Without one, the shop sharpens to its standard, and that can differ from
shop to shop.

A sharpening costs 8 to 12 euros depending on the shop and takes a few minutes. The first
sharpening of new steel costs around 20 euros, because it needs more passes. If possible,
always go to the same sharpener: machines and habits differ, and a consistent result is worth
more than saving a few euros.

After sharpening, both edges must be the **same height**. Otherwise your child pulls to one
side. You can hardly see it by eye. The sharpener checks it with an **edge checker**
(Kantenprüfer), a special measuring tool – here you have to trust the shop. One more reason
to always go to the same sharpener. If your child pulls to one side after sharpening, tell
the sharpener.

<Callout type="tipp" title="Profiling, pitch and replacement steel">
The hollow isn't the only curve on a blade, and on most skates today the steel can be
swapped. That's covered in the article on
<a href={href('/en/skates')}>blades, profiling and replacement steel</a>. For U13 the short
version is: hands off, clean edges are enough.
</Callout>

## Sharpening at home

Sharpening machines for home use have got really good in recent years. A well-known example
is the sharpener from **Sparx**. A European supplier is **ProSharp** from Sweden, which has
belonged to Bauer since 2021. We name them so you know what to ask about – this is not a
buying recommendation.

Instead of a grinding wheel, Sparx uses **grinding rings**. Each ring has a fixed radius: for
5/8" you need a 5/8" ring. The flat FIRE grinds from above also run on their own rings.

**What it costs.** A starter set costs around 1300 euros. It includes a grinding ring, a
honing stone for the burr on normal steel and an edge checker. For coated steel you need a
special honing stone, which costs around another 25 euros.

Each additional grinding ring costs around 80 euros.
[According to Sparx](https://help.sparxhockey.com/en-US/how-many-sharpenings-do-each-grinding-ring-provide-506799)
a ring lasts for about 40 to 60 sharpenings –
[fewer](https://blogs.sparxhockey.com/pages/faq) if the blades are very dull before they are
sharpened. That's 1.50 to 2 euros per sharpening. At 10 to 15 sharpenings per season, a ring
therefore lasts three to five seasons.

For comparison: a child who has their skates sharpened every two to three weeks pays roughly
100 to 180 euros per season in the shop. For one family on its own, the machine only pays
for itself after many years – shared, it looks quite different.

**Together instead of alone.** Some clubs have their own machine. Other parents buy one
together. What has worked well is that everyone shares the machine, but each family buys its
**own grinding ring**. That way every child is sharpened to their own radius, and each family
only wears out its own ring.

If you sharpen yourself, you also take over what the sharpener otherwise does. After
sharpening, a fine metal burr is left on the edges. You remove it with the honing stone – on
coated steel with the special stone. Then you use the edge checker to see whether both edges
are the same height, and write down the radius that works.

<Callout type="merke" title="Rules of thumb, not laws">
Shops don't always name radii the same way, and ice quality differs from rink to rink. Take
the numbers here as a starting point, try things out in small steps, and write down what
worked for your child.
</Callout>
