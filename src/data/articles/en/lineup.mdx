Before every game there is a lineup (Aufstellung). It says who is on the ice with whom, and
in which position. To parents it first looks like a list full of abbreviations. This article
explains how it is built and the ideas behind it.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
First come **three forward lines** (LW, C, RW), then **three defence pairs** (LD, RD), then
the **two goalies**. The players in a line should be at a similar level. The **first line
is not automatically the best**, and the **C** on a jersey doesn't mean "best player". **All
three lines play**, in turn – on the penalty kill and power play too. The two goalies play
**30 minutes each**. Over their two years in U13, every child learns **every position**.
</Callout>

## Where to find the lineup

The lineup first appears in **TeamPlus** (formerly SpielerPlus), the app the team is
organised through. It can still change until shortly before the game. Someone is ill,
someone arrives late, or something else doesn't fit.

In the locker room and at the bench it then hangs on a whiteboard or on paper. For the
board, every child has a magnet with their name on it. That way the lineup can be changed
quickly during the game. What is on the board counts.

## How a lineup is built

Each team has five skaters and one goalie on the ice. The skaters change all the time. A
group that goes onto the ice together is called a **line**. One turn on the ice is called a
**shift**; it usually lasts only 40 to 60 seconds.

The lineup always follows the same order:

1. **Three forward lines.** Each has three forwards: left, centre, right.
2. **Three defence pairs.** Each has two defenders: left and right.
3. **Two goalies.** The one listed first starts the game.

The abbreviations are used like this everywhere in ice hockey:

| D 1, D 2, D 3 | Defence pair 1, 2, 3 | Verteidigerpaar 1, 2, 3 |

A **C** next to a name marks the captain, an **A** the assistants. More on that below.

## Who plays when

We usually bring **15 skaters and 2 goalies** to a game, "15+2" for short. That is exactly
three forward lines and three defence pairs.

**All three lines play.** They go on in turn: 1, 2, 3, then 1 again. No line sits on the
bench longer because it is supposedly weaker.

**The goalies share the game.** Each plays 30 minutes, so half of it. The one listed first in
the lineup starts.

<Callout type="achtung" title="The only exception">
If the game is close in the **last 5 minutes**, the **10 players who are playing best that
day** may go on more often. That might be enough to win a tight game. It is the exception.
Normally the rule is: we play to develop the children, not to win at any price.
</Callout>

## Forwards and defence change separately

The forward lines and the defence pairs are two separate lists. They don't change at the
same time. Defenders often stay out a little longer, or they change while the forwards are
still attacking.

So defence pair 1 is not tied to forward line 1. Over the course of a game it plays with
line 1, then line 2, then line 3. We try to reset that now and then, for example at a
stoppage. It never stays perfectly in step, though – and that's fine.

## Line changes: how it works at the bench

A line change has to be quick, otherwise the opponent briefly has an extra skater. That's
why, before every game, we run through several scenarios during the **off-ice warm-up**,
like a simulation. The children practise normal line changes, PK1 and PK2, a penalty
against the centre and other situations. On the ice, they then already know when they are
up.

### Know your line

Every child has to know **which line** they play in. After a stoppage, the coach may call
out "Line 1!" ("Reihe 1!"). Then line 1 goes on the ice: forward line 1 and defence pair
D 1.

If defence pair D 1 has only just come off and is still tired, D 2 goes instead.

<Callout type="tipp" title="Know who plays before you">
In normal play, it is enough to know who plays your position in the line **before you**.
When that player comes off the ice, you go on. So if you are LW in line 2, you wait for the
LW of line 1.

For the **penalty kill**, you also need to know who plays your position in the **other two
lines**.
</Callout>

### On the bench

Forwards and defenders sit separately. With every change, everyone shuffles a bit further:
the **forwards always towards the door**, the **defenders towards their own goal**. So the
forward line that is up next sits closest to the door – in period 2 right behind the
defenders. That way it is always clear who is up next.

The defenders sit as close as possible to their **own goal**. That way, after a change,
they get to where they are needed fastest. Because the teams switch ends after every
period, the places switch too:

- **Periods 1 and 3:** the forwards sit next to the door, the defenders at the other end of
  the bench.
- **Period 2:** the other way round. The defenders sit next to the door, the forwards at
  the other end.

<BenchSeating />

### At the door

- The **defenders** jump over the boards.
- The **backup goalie** works the door and lets players out and in.
- Players going **onto the ice** have right of way and go first.
- Players coming **off the ice** wait until everyone is out. Only then do they go onto the
  bench. That way nobody blocks the way onto the ice.

## How the lines are put together

The most important idea: the children **within a line** should be at roughly the same level
and able to play well together.

If the gap is too big, it gets hard for everyone. The strongest child then does everything
alone, because they don't trust the others with a pass. The others just wait and hardly get
the puck. Nobody learns much. Children at a similar level pass to each other, and every one
of them gets into the game.

### The first line is not the best line

"Line 1" is a number, not a ranking. It only says which line goes on first. Good players are
often deliberately placed in line 2 or 3, or on defence. Whether your child is in line 1 or
line 3 says nothing about how good they are.

### Captain and assistants

The **C** (captain) and the **A**s (assistants) don't necessarily go to the most skilled
players or the top scorers. They go to children who are the **soul of the team**. They take
on responsibility, lead by example and look after the others.

They are also the **link** between the team and the coaches. They represent the team and
carry concerns from the players to the coaches and back. On the ice, they are the only
players allowed to speak to the referee.

## Everyone plays every position

During their two years in U13, every child learns **every position**. A forward who has
played defence understands better where to skate – and the other way round. That way the
children become well-rounded players who understand the game as a whole.

So your child won't always play the same position. That is on purpose, not a punishment.
The positions differ in how hard they are:

### Wing: the way in

On the **wing** (LW, RW) the tasks are the clearest. A winger mostly stays on their side of
the ice. In defence they cover the opposing defender at the blue line. In attack they look
for open space and a shot. That makes the wing the easiest position to start with.

### Defence: the fourth forward

On **defence** (LD, RD) you are the last skater in front of your own goal. Everyone sees
mistakes here, and they quickly lead to a goal against. On top of that comes skating
backwards.

Some of the strongest players are deliberately placed on defence. That makes the defence
reliable. And it means there are players who carry the puck out of their own zone
themselves, or play a good **first pass** – the pass that starts the attack.

Modern defenders don't only defend, though. They are called the **fourth forward**: they
join the attack, keep the puck in the zone at the blue line and shoot from there.

### Centre: the hardest position

The **centre** (C) has the most to do. They take the **faceoff** (Bully), the puck drop
that restarts play after every stoppage. In attack they connect the two wingers.

In defence they are almost a third defender. They work deep in their own zone together with
the defenders and protect the **slot**, the area right in front of the goal where most goals
are scored from. So a centre must know where they belong in every situation – that is why
it is the hardest position.

## Penalty kill and power play: PK and PP

When a player gets a penalty, they sit in the penalty box. Their team then plays one skater
short for a while: the **penalty kill**, or **PK** (Unterzahl).

On the penalty kill there are only four skaters on the ice. A penalty-kill unit is made up
of:

- the **centre** of a forward line,
- **one winger** from the same line – LW or RW, the two decide that between themselves,
- **two defenders** (LD and RD).

If a **centre** is in the penalty box, **both wingers** of their line play instead, together
with the two defenders.

If a **defender** is in the penalty box, the other defenders on their side rotate as usual –
just two of them instead of three, until the penalty is over. For example, if the LD of D 1
is in the box, the LDs of D 2 and D 3 take turns on the left.

There is no separate penalty-kill team. The normal order applies here too: the line whose
turn it is plays **PK1**, the first penalty-kill unit. The next line plays **PK2** and
replaces it. If line 3 is up, then line 3 plays PK1.

It works the same the other way round. When the opponent gets a penalty, your team has one
skater more: the **power play**, or **PP** (Überzahl). The line whose turn it is plays
**PP1**, the next one **PP2**.

That way **every line** plays the penalty kill and the power play. That is part of learning
every game situation too.

## The sixth skater

Sometimes a team pulls its goalie off the ice and sends an extra skater on instead. That
happens, for example, when the team is one goal down just before the end, or during a
delayed penalty against the opponent. This player is called the **sixth skater** or
**extra attacker**.

In our team, that is always the **centre of the next line**. So nobody has to look for who
goes. If line 1 is on the ice, the centre of line 2 jumps on.
