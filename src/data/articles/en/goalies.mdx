The goalie is the only player who stays on the ice for the whole game. We bring two to every
game, and each plays 30 minutes (see <a href={href('/en/lineup')}>Lineup</a>). This article
is for our goalies (Torhüter) and their parents. It shows what you learn at U13. Further
down there is a section for everyone else: what teammates can do for the goalie.

It is based on the goaltending page of the
<a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/grundlagentraining/torwartspiel">DEB training framework</a>
for U13. Its motto: consolidate the basics and **spark enthusiasm**. Stopping pucks
should be fun.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
- **Skater first, then goalie.** Good skating is the basis for everything.
- **Nose and belly button to the puck.** The goalie stands on the line from the puck to the
  middle of the goal.
- **Never take your eyes off the puck**, before, during and after the save.
- **The butterfly is a tool**, not the answer to every shot.
- **After a goal against, the next shot is what counts.**
</Callout>

## Skater first, then goalie

A goalie moves all game long: sideways, forwards, back, down to the knees and up again.
That is why goalies should be among the **best skaters on the team**. Forwards, backwards,
crossovers, stopping: they learn it like everyone else.

On top of that come three movements only goalies use:

- **C-cut**: from the stance, one leg pushes a half circle like a "C" into the ice. This is
  how the goalie moves forwards and back without losing the stance.
- **Shuffle**: small steps sideways, both skates stay turned to the puck. For short
  distances, for example when the puck slowly moves along the boards.
- **T-push**: the front skate turns in the direction you want to go, the back skate pushes
  off. From above it looks like a "T". For long distances, for example when a pass comes
  across in front of the goal.

The DEB warns against focusing on goalie skating too early. It makes you less agile. At
U13 the focus is still on general skating (see also
<a href={href('/en/foundation-training')}>Foundation training</a>).

## The stance

The **stance** (Grundstellung) is the position every save starts from:

- Feet a little wider than the shoulders
- Knees and ankles bent
- Weight on the balls of the feet, skates slightly on the inside edges
- Upper body leaning slightly forward
- Hands in front of the body, pointing at the puck
- Stick in front of the body, on the ice

Important: every body is built differently. The stance doesn't have to look the same on
everyone. It has to be comfortable and allow quick moves in every direction.

## Angle: nose and belly button to the puck

The shooter sees an area of the goal between the two posts. The goalie wants to cover as
much of it as possible. To do that, they stand on the **line from the puck to the middle
of the goal**. This position is called the **angle** (Winkel).

The DEB's rule of thumb for it: **nose and belly button point at the puck.** If the goalie
also faces the puck straight on (*square*), both posts are covered.

What matters is the **puck**, not the player. The puck is often beside the opponent, on
the side of their stick. The shot comes from where the puck is.

## Depth: how far out?

Besides the angle there is **depth**: how far in front of the goal does the goalie stand?
The further out, the less the shooter sees of the goal. But the longer the way to the side
when a pass comes.

So it helps to **count** early: how many opponents are coming?

- **One against the goalie**, or one opponent against one defender: the goalie can be
  bold. Rule of thumb: out to the edge of the blue crease.
- **Two against one**: the goalie stays deeper in the crease. When the pass comes across,
  the way to the other side is short.

## Seeing the puck

Every save starts with the eyes. The puck has to be in sight **before, during and after**
the save. This is called *tracking*. A good sentence to remember: watch the puck. Always
and everywhere.

Sometimes someone is in the way. This is called **traffic**. Opponents stand in front of
the goal on purpose to block the goalie's view. But **our own players** are often in the
way too. For U13 the DEB's rule is: **"Look for the line of sight!"** (Sichtlinie suchen).
The goalie moves their head and body until they see the puck.

You can also read when a shot is coming. Typical signs: the shooter stops moving the puck
back and forth, stops skating, lifts their head, draws the stick back and leans forward.

## Making saves

A goalie has several tools. At U13 they learn which one fits when:

- **Catch glove** (Fanghand): it catches high shots. A caught puck gives no rebound. The
  catch glove should play an important role from the start.
- **Blocker** (Stockhand, the glove on the stick hand): it steers the puck **into the
  corner**, away from the goal. Not just letting it bounce off, but sending it to the side
  on purpose.
- **Stick**: low shots are stopped with the stick. This should become a habit.
- **Butterfly**: the goalie drops to the knees, the leg pads lie flat and wide on the ice.
  This covers the whole bottom of the goal. The upper body stays upright and leans
  slightly forward, hands in front of the body, stick on the ice.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Not the butterfly on every shot">
The butterfly is a tool for **blocking**, mainly on low and mid-height shots. Anyone who
drops to their knees on every shot opens the top of the goal and is slower to get back up.
The DEB says so explicitly: at U13 the goalie should not drop into the butterfly on every
shot without a reason.
</Callout>

Right at the post there are further techniques with names like **VH** and **RVH**. They
help when the puck comes from the side or from behind the goal. Goalies get to know them at
U13, but they shouldn't happen automatically yet. **Reading the game** comes first: where
is the puck, who can shoot, who can pass?

## After the shot

After the save the work isn't over. The puck often bounces off and lies in front of the
goal. This is a **rebound**. Nobody manages zero rebounds. What matters is what happens
next:

- **Follow the puck.** The eyes stay on the puck, after the save too.
- **Be ready again quickly.** Get up, turn to the puck, back onto the line to the puck.
  This is called **recovery** (Nachabwehr).
- **If the puck lies free in front of the goal**, the goalie can push it away with the
  stick or cover it.

## Playing the puck

A goalie is also a player with a stick. At U13 they learn to **stop, carry and pass** the
puck, on the forehand and the backhand.

An example: the opponents rim the puck along the boards behind our goal. The goalie skates
over, stops it and leaves it for our defender or passes it on. Before that, they check over
their shoulder: where are the opponents?

For this to work, goalie and defenders have to **agree beforehand**. Which calls do we use?
Who takes the puck? That belongs in practice, not only in the game. And goalies should be
allowed to try what they learned in games, even if it goes wrong sometimes.

## After a goal against

Every goalie lets in goals, even the best in the world. What matters is what happens next.
The <a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/einfuehrung/torwartspiel">DEB's introduction to
goaltending</a> describes a short routine:

1. Get the puck out of the net.
2. Skate to the boards for a moment.
3. Breathe deeply, take a sip.
4. Think: **the next shot is mine.**

The routine is the same every time. It helps you put the goal behind you. What went wrong
is looked at later in practice, not in the middle of the game.

And: **never blame your teammates.** A goal against almost always has several reasons.

Goalies need patience. <a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/einfuehrung/torwartspiel">According
to the DEB</a>, they are fully trained only at about 23 on average. At U13 it is about the
basics and the joy of playing in goal.

## What teammates can do

The goalie doesn't stop pucks alone. Every skater can help:

- **Stay out of the line of sight.** When the puck is at the blue line, stand beside the
  line between the puck and the goalie, not in it.
- **Clear rebounds.** If the puck lies in front of the goal after a save, the nearest
  defender gets there first and plays it out of the danger zone.
- **Don't leave opponents alone in front of the goal.** Stay on the
  <a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a> so they don't stand free in
  front of the goalie.
- **Listen and talk.** When the goalie plays the puck behind the net, goalie and defenders
  agree on who does what.
- **After a goal against, skate over to the goalie** for a quick tap. No blame.

## Equipment

Goalies need their own gear. The terms you hear in a German shop:

| Piece | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Mask** (Maske) | Goalie mask with a cage. Protects head and face. |
| **Neck guard** (Halsschutz) | Goalie neck guard, ideally with protection for the upper chest. |
| **Chest protector** (Brustschutz) | Protects the upper body and arms. |
| **Catch glove** (Fanghand) | The glove that catches. |
| **Blocker** (Stockhand) | The glove with the board on it. |
| **Leg pads** (Schoner) | The big leg pads. |
| **Goalie pants** (Torwarthose) | Like hockey pants, but with more padding. |
| **Goalie skates** (Torwartschlittschuhe) | Lower, with a protective shell around the boot. |
| **Goalie stick** (Torwartschläger) | With a wide shaft at the bottom, the *paddle*. |

According to the <a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/grundlagentraining/materialkunde">DEB</a>,
goalies at U13 should have their own goalie pants, a neck guard, a mask and goalie skates.
And more and more, they put on the gear by themselves.

What to look for when trying gear on (from the
<a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/basisschulung/materialkunde">DEB equipment guide</a>):

- **Leg pads**: they reach about the middle of the thigh. The knee sits exactly on the spot
  made for it. Don't strap them too tight, or the child can't move well. Many shops measure
  from the ankle to the knee for this.
- **Catch glove and blocker**: sized to the hand. The child must be able to close the catch
  glove completely.
- **Chest protector**: covers the whole upper body and the arms.
- **Mask**: sits firmly, doesn't wobble, the child sees well downwards and to the side.
  Don't save money on the mask: it protects the head.

**Cost.** Goalie gear is much more expensive than a skater's. New, a complete junior set
costs roughly 1,000 to 1,800 euros. Used gear is much cheaper, and for children who grow
quickly that is perfectly fine. Many clubs lend goalie gear for the start. Ask the club
before you buy.

The same rules as for everyone apply to skates and sharpening:
<a href={href('/en/skates')}>Skates</a> and <a href={href('/en/sharpening')}>Sharpening</a>.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents in the stands">
Goals are scored, not let in. After the game, don't ask about the goals against. Ask
instead: Was it fun? What went well? What did you learn today? And at the next game, watch
only the goalie for a while: do they follow the puck? Do they stand on the line to the puck?
Are they ready again quickly after a rebound?
</Callout>
