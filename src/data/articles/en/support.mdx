The third call from the bench is **“Support!”**. German hockey uses the English word. The
idea is simple: whoever has the puck or is fighting for it should never be alone.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
**In defence:** two to the puck. One pins the opponent against the boards, the second
takes the puck. **In attack:** one teammate is **near** the puck carrier, a few metres
away and open for a pass, often on the boards. Other teammates are **far** away and offer
a second option. If the puck carrier is stuck: **go and help** – don’t wait in front of
the goal for the pass.
</Callout>

## Why support matters so much

One player alone against two opponents nearly always loses the puck. Two against one
nearly always win it. Support means turning a 1‑on‑1 or a 1‑on‑2 into a **2‑on‑1**.

Our <a href={href('/en/season-concept')}>season concept</a> says we never just whack the
puck away blindly. That only works if the player with the puck has someone to give it to.
That someone is the support.

## Support in defence

When the opponent has the puck, support means: the player putting pressure on the puck
gets help. In <a href={href('/en/coverage')}>Zuordnung</a> these are the first two jobs:
pressure on the puck and low support.

### Overload: two to the puck

To **overload** means: on the puck side there are more of us than of them. The defender
comes from below, from the goal side. The centre comes from above or from the inside. The
opponent with the puck has no free way out.

<RinkPlay play="overload" locale="en" />

Important: **only two** go to the puck. The others stay in the middle: one in front of the
goal, one in the high slot, one between the puck and the blue line. A third player at the
puck is missing in front of the goal.

### Pinning: hold them and let a teammate take the puck

To **pin** means holding the opponent against the boards with your body. The player skates
up close, knees bent, and presses them against the boards with hip and shoulder. The
opponent can neither get away nor get to the puck.

But whoever is pinning can’t play the puck themselves. They need a second player to take
the puck. So pinning **only works with support**.

<RinkPlay play="pin" locale="en" />

<Callout type="achtung" title="Pinning is not a check">
You don’t skate into the opponent with a run-up. No shove, no hands, no stick against the
body – that is a penalty. Whoever pins holds the opponent until the teammate has the puck.
Only then do they let go and skate to their area.
</Callout>

Once the second player has the puck, the game turns around. Now **they** need support: a
passing option before the next opponent arrives. That is what the next part is about.

## Support in attack

When we have the puck, the player with it is the **puck carrier**. Support now means: the
puck carrier always has at least one way to pass the puck. There are two kinds of support
for that.

### Near support

**Near support** is a teammate a few metres from the puck carrier. The puck carrier can get
rid of the puck to them quickly, with a short, safe pass. Near support is often on the
boards.

A typical example is the breakout from our own zone. The defender gets the puck in the
corner, an opponent is coming. The winger is on the boards, roughly level with the faceoff
dots, and open. One short pass and the pressure is gone.

<RinkPlay play="near-far" locale="en" />

Good near support has four things:

- **Open.** No opponent between you and the puck. If you can’t see the puck carrier’s
  blade, someone is in the way. Take a step or two to the side.
- **Close, but not too close.** A short pass has to do. Stand right next to them and one
  opponent can bother you both.
- **Ready.** Stick on the ice, the blade shows where the pass should go. Calling helps.
- **Moving.** Skate towards the pass. That makes it shorter and safer.

### When the puck carrier is stuck

Support is needed most when the puck carrier is **under pressure**: two opponents push them
against the boards, or one pins them. Then they have no time to look around. A long pass to
the front of the goal hardly ever gets through.

Now the near support has to **go there**. A teammate comes within a few metres, into the
open gap next to the battle. The puck carrier can push the puck along the boards to them
with the stick or the skate. Or the teammate picks up the loose puck. A 1‑on‑2 becomes a
2‑on‑2.

<RinkPlay play="pressure-support" locale="en" />

<Callout type="tipp" title="Don’t wait in front of the goal">
Goals are scored in front of the goal. That is why children like to wait there for the
pass. But first the puck has to get out of the corner. As long as your teammate is fighting
two opponents there alone, no pass is coming. Go and help – the goal comes after.
</Callout>

### Far support

**Far support** is further away. It is the second option when the near one is covered.
Typical far support is:

- the **other defender**, on the far side of the goal or at the blue line,
- the **weak-side winger**, high on the far boards,
- a player skating into an open gap in the **slot**.

Far support is how play switches sides. The opponents have focused on the puck side, so
there is often a lot of space on the other side.

One player in the slot is good. But they keep moving and look for the gap between the
opponents. Two or three standing in front of the goal waiting help nobody.

### The triangle

The puck carrier, near support and far support together form a **triangle**. Then the
puck carrier always has two options. And whoever gets the puck immediately has two again.
That is why players skate so much: the triangle moves with the puck.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting in front of the goal** while the teammate is alone in the corner.
- **Standing behind an opponent.** Then no pass is possible.
- **Too close.** Two of our players on one spot get in each other’s way.
- **Standing still.** A player standing still is easy to cover.
- **Trying to take the puck yourself while pinning.** Then the opponent is free again.
  Whoever pins waits for the support.
- **Three to the puck.** Support means helping, not everyone going there. The others stay
  in their area.

<Callout type="merke" title="Rules of thumb, not set plays">
“A few metres”, “level with the faceoff dots”, “two to the puck”: these are guideline
values. The drawings show examples, not fixed plays. In a game every situation is a little
different. The idea always stays the same: nobody is left alone with the puck.
</Callout>
