Do you know the game of tag? If only one player is "it", the others get away easily. But if
one hunts and a second blocks the escape route, it gets tight. That is exactly how the
forecheck works: **hunting as a team.**

It is about two English words that German hockey uses too:

- **Forecheck** means: the opponents have the puck in **their** zone. We put pressure on
  them there and win it back.
- **Backcheck** means: we have lost the puck. Now we skate back as fast as we can and
  protect our goal.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
**Forecheck:** the **dog** hunts the puck carrier. The **fox** closes the boards. The
**hawk** stays high in the middle. Two at the puck, one covering.
**Backcheck:** dog and fox become **cheetahs** and sprint back through the middle to the
“house” in front of our goal. The hawk helps the defenders.
</Callout>

## Four animals, four jobs

So everyone can remember the jobs, each job gets an animal. The picture comes from North
America.

<AnimalCards />

Important: the animals are **not positions**. A winger isn’t always the dog. The dog is
whoever gets to the puck **first**. That is why everyone has to be able to be every animal.

## Forecheck: the three doors

Imagine the opponent with the puck in the corner is standing in a room. The room has
**three doors** they can play the puck out through:

1. **up the boards** to their winger,
2. **into the middle** to their centre,
3. **behind the goal** to their other defender.

On the forecheck we close the doors. The fox closes door 1, the hawk door 2. The dog hunts
the puck carrier and gives them no time to think. Only door 3 is left – and then the animals
simply swap roles.

Look out for two things:

- **After the pass behind the goal**, a different player is closest. They become the dog.
  The other two fill the free roles: one to the boards, one high.
- **At the end**, the opponent plays the puck up the boards. The fox is already waiting
  there. Once we win the puck, the forecheck is over and we attack. The DEB says: in the
  attacking zone, one player is always **in front of the opponents’ goal**.

### The dog: herd, don’t bite

The dog is a **sheepdog**. A sheepdog doesn’t bite the sheep. It drives them where it wants
them: towards the fence. For us, the fence is the boards.

- **Get there fast**, but slow down just before. Arriving at full speed, you simply get
  skated around.
- **Come from the inside**, from the middle. Then the puck carrier’s only way is to the
  boards. It is the same idea as the <a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a>.
- **Stick on the puck.** The stick is on the ice, between the puck and the middle.

A **body check**, a deliberate hit with the body, is not allowed at U13. The dog works with
the stick, the skating line and the body. It may **pin** the opponent against the boards,
holding them there. That is in the <a href={href('/en/support')}>Support</a> article.

### The fox: wait at the mouse hole

A fox doesn’t chase the mouse. It waits at the hole the mouse **has to** come out of. So
our fox does **not** skate at the puck carrier too. It skates to the boards on the puck
side, between the puck and the next opponent further up.

Under pressure, the puck carrier nearly always plays the puck up the boards. That is exactly
where the fox is waiting. Or the dog pokes the puck loose, and the fox picks it up.

### The hawk: circle up high

A hawk circles high up. From there it sees everything. It only swoops when it pays off. Our
hawk stays **high in the middle**, between the faceoff circles and the blue line.

- If the opponents play the puck into the middle, the hawk is there.
- If the puck gets out of the corner after all, the hawk is the first one back.
- If the dog wins the puck, the hawk is open for a pass.

The **defenders** stay at the blue line. They keep the puck in the zone and are ready to
skate back.

### Do – and don’t

The most common forecheck mistake: everyone goes to the puck. It looks hard-working, but
then all three doors are open.

## Forecheck is support

The forecheck is closely tied to <a href={href('/en/support')}>support</a>:

- **Dog and fox** are two at the puck. A 1‑on‑1 becomes a 2‑on‑1. That is the
  **overload** from the Support article. The fox is the dog’s near support.
- **The hawk** is the far support. It keeps the space behind the two covered.

The rule from the Support article applies here too: **only two are at the puck, and only
one attacks.** The dog hunts, the fox waits on the boards. A third player at the puck is
missing in the middle, and a single pass beats all three.

## Backcheck: the cheetah

When the puck is gone, everything turns around. Now the opponents attack. Whoever is faster
now wins the race to the goal. That is why dog and fox become **cheetahs**. The cheetah is
the fastest animal on land. It sprints short and at full power.

<RinkStory story="backcheck" client:visible locale="en" />

**The hawk helps the defenders.** It was high in the middle, so it is closest to our goal.
It skates back inside the puck carrier, on the
<a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a>. That way the puck carrier has no
way into the middle.

**The cheetahs** sprint back. Four rules apply:

- **Through the middle**, not along the boards. The middle is the shortest way to the goal.
- **To the house.** The “house” is the space in front of our goal, between the faceoff
  dots. Most goals are scored there.
- **Stop.** Stop in the house, don’t glide past in a curve. A player who has stopped can
  start in any direction at once. A player gliding past has to turn round first.
- **Shoulder check.** Like on a bike before turning: a quick look over your shoulder. Where
  is the puck? Where is an opponent without the puck? Looking shows you whom to pick up.

Once everyone is in our own zone, each player takes their area. How that works is in the
<a href={href('/en/coverage')}>Coverage</a> article.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents in the stands">
After we lose the puck, don’t watch the puck. Watch the forwards who were deep in the
attacking zone. Are they sprinting back through the middle? Then they are cheetahs – even
if they never touch the puck afterwards.
</Callout>

## Other names for the same thing

The three forecheck roles have many names. They all mean the same thing:

- **F1, F2, F3.** In English-speaking hockey the three forwards on the forecheck are simply
  “Forward 1, 2 and 3”. The DEB writes **S1, S2** and **S3**, for Stürmer (forward).
- **Bull instead of dog.** Some call the first player the bull, because it charges at the
  puck.
- **Pressure, support, cover.** The first puts on pressure, the second supports, the third
  covers.
- **2‑1‑2.** This counts the players from front to back: two deep at the puck, one high in
  the middle, two defenders at the blue line.

It doesn’t matter which picture you use at home. What matters is that everyone knows what
to do as first, second or third player.

## Common mistakes

- **Everyone to the puck.** Three forwards in the corner, and one pass beats them all.
- **The dog skates past.** At full speed you are easy to go around. Slow down just before.
- **The fox skates at the puck carrier too.** Then door 1 is open.
- **The hawk goes deep.** Then door 2 is open, and nobody is first back.
- **Skating back on the outside.** Along the boards the way is longer, and the middle stays
  open.
- **Gliding instead of sprinting.** A backcheck is a sprint, not a roll-out.

## For parents: what the DEB says

This article builds on the <a href={href('/en/foundation-training')}>DEB foundation
training</a>, the page <a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/grundlagentraining/offensive-zone">Offensive
Zone</a> on deb-rtk.de (in German).

There the DEB describes a **general forecheck**, a simple basic shape: the two wingers play
a bit deeper. On the puck side, the winger attacks the puck carrier. The third forward stays
a bit higher and waits, so they can react to anything.

For the **backcheck** the DEB says: if we lose the puck in the opponents’ third, the high
forward helps the defenders. The other two skate back fast through the middle to the house
and stop. On the way they look over their shoulder.

These are **principles**, not set plays. At this age the DEB wants no systems learnt by
heart. The children should understand why they stand where they stand. The animals help
with that.

<Callout type="merke" title="Rules of thumb, not set plays">
“Two at the puck, one high” and “through the middle to the house” are rules of thumb. The
drawings show examples, not fixed plays. In a game every situation is a bit different. The
idea always stays the same: pressure on the puck, but never everyone at once – and after
losing the puck, back fast.
</Callout>
