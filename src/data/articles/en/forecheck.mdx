When the opponents have the puck, we want it back. Two English words describe how, and
German hockey uses them too:

- **Forecheck** means putting pressure on the opponents where they build their attack.
  Mostly that is in **their** zone, so in our attacking zone.
- **Backcheck** means skating back fast after losing the puck, to protect our own goal.

Both are part of the <a href={href('/en/foundation-training')}>DEB foundation training</a>,
on the page <a href="https://www.deb-rtk.de/grundlagentraining/offensive-zone">Offensive
Zone</a> on deb-rtk.de (in German). The drawings in this article build on the DEB’s.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
**Forecheck:** the **dog** attacks the puck carrier. The **fox** closes the boards on the
puck side. The **hawk** stays high in the middle. Two at the puck, one covering.
**Backcheck:** the hawk helps the defenders. Dog and fox become **cheetahs**: they sprint
back through the middle to the “house” in front of our goal.
</Callout>

## What the DEB says

The DEB describes a **general forecheck**, a simple basic shape. The two wingers play a bit
deeper, closer to the opponents’ goal. On the side where the puck is, the winger attacks the
puck carrier. The third forward stays a bit higher and waits. That way they can react,
whatever the opponents do.

For the **backcheck** the DEB says: if we lose the puck in the opponents’ third, the high
forward helps the defenders. The other two forwards skate back fast **through the middle**
to the house and stop. On the way they always look over their shoulder.

These are **principles**, not set plays. At this age the DEB wants no systems learnt by
heart. The children should understand why they stand where they stand.

## Forecheck: dog, fox and hawk

To help children remember the roles, there is a well-known picture from North America:
three animals. Each one has a job.

First, one term: the **strong side** is the side where the puck is. The other side is the
**weak side**.

### The dog: pressure on the puck

The dog is the first player at the puck. It hunts the puck carrier and gives them no time.

- **Get there fast**, but don’t crash in blindly. Just before the opponent, slow down and
  arrive under control.
- **Come from the inside.** The dog pushes the puck carrier to the boards, away from the
  middle. It is the same idea as the <a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a>.
- **Stick on the puck.** The stick is on the ice, between the puck and the middle.

Body checking is not allowed at U13. The dog works with the stick, the skating line and body
position. It may **pin** the opponent against the boards, as described in the
<a href={href('/en/support')}>Support</a> article.

### The fox: close the boards

The fox is the second player. It does **not** skate at the puck carrier too. It skates to
the boards on the strong side, between the puck and the next opponent further up.

Under pressure, the puck carrier nearly always plays the puck up along the boards. That is
exactly where the fox is waiting. It grabs the puck when it comes. Or it picks up the loose
puck once the dog has poked it free.

### The hawk: stay high

The hawk is the third player. It stays **high in the middle**, between the faceoff circles
and the blue line. From there it sees everything, like a hawk from above.

- If the opponents play the puck into the middle, the hawk is there.
- If the puck comes out of the corner, the hawk is the first one back.
- If the dog wins the puck, the hawk is an open passing option in the slot.

The **defenders** stay at the blue line. They keep the puck in the zone and are ready to
skate back.

### The roles change

The animals are **not positions**. The dog is whoever gets to the puck first. That can be
the winger, the centre or now and then a defender.

If the opponents move the puck behind the goal to the other side, the roles change. Whoever
is now closest becomes the dog. The next one closes the boards, the third goes high. That is
why every player has to know every role.

## Forecheck is support

For us, the forecheck is closely tied to <a href={href('/en/support')}>support</a>:

- **Dog and fox** are two at the puck. A 1‑on‑1 becomes a 2‑on‑1. That is the
  **overload** from the Support article. The fox is the dog’s near support.
- **The hawk** is the far support. It keeps the space behind the two covered. And it
  covers in case the opponents get through after all.

The rule from the Support article applies here too: **only two go to the puck.** A third
player at the puck doesn’t help. They are missing in the middle, and a single pass beats
all three.

Once we win the puck, the forecheck is over. Now we attack. The DEB says: in the attacking
zone, one player is always **in front of the opponents’ goal**.

## Other pictures for the forecheck

The three roles have many names. They all mean the same thing:

- **F1, F2, F3.** In English-speaking hockey the three forwards on the forecheck are simply
  “Forward 1, 2 and 3”. The DEB writes **S1, S2** and **S3**, for Stürmer (forward).
- **Bull instead of dog.** Some call the first player the bull, because it charges at the
  puck.
- **Pressure, support, cover.** The first puts on pressure, the second supports, the third
  covers.
- **2‑1‑2.** This counts the players from front to back: two deep at the puck, one high in
  the middle, two defenders at the blue line.

It doesn’t matter which picture you use at home. What matters is that everyone knows what
to do as first, second or third player.

## Backcheck: the cheetah

When the puck is gone, everything turns around. Now the opponents attack, and we have to be
back faster than they are. The backcheck animal is the **cheetah**, the fastest animal on
land.

<RinkPlay play="backcheck-house" locale="en" />

**The hawk helps the defenders.** It was high in the middle, so it is closest to our goal.
It skates back inside the puck carrier, on the
<a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a>. That way the puck carrier has no
way into the middle.

**Dog and fox become cheetahs.** They were deep in the attacking zone. Now they sprint back:

- **Through the middle**, not along the boards. The shortest way to the goal is the middle.
- **To the house.** The “house” is the space in front of our goal, between the faceoff dots.
- **Stop.** Stop in the house, don’t glide past in a curve. A player who has stopped can
  start in any direction at once.
- **Look over your shoulder.** Where is the puck? Where is an opponent without the puck?
  Looking lets you cut out a pass and see whom you have to pick up.

Once everyone is in our own zone, each player takes their area. How that works is in the
<a href={href('/en/coverage')}>Coverage</a> article.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents in the stands">
After we lose the puck, don’t watch the puck. Watch the forwards who were deep in the
attacking zone. Are they sprinting back through the middle? Then they are cheetahs – even
if they never touch the puck afterwards.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- **Everyone to the puck.** Three forwards in the corner, and one pass beats them all.
- **The dog skates past.** Arriving at full speed makes you easy to go around. Slow down
  just before.
- **The fox skates at the puck carrier too.** Then the boards are open.
- **The hawk goes deep.** Then the middle is empty, and nobody is first back.
- **Skating back on the outside.** Along the boards the way is longer, and the middle stays
  open.
- **Gliding instead of sprinting.** A backcheck is a sprint, not a roll-out.

<Callout type="merke" title="Rules of thumb, not set plays">
“Two at the puck, one high” and “through the middle to the house” are rules of thumb. The
drawings show examples, not fixed plays. In a game every situation is a bit different. The
idea always stays the same: pressure on the puck, but never everyone at once – and after
losing the puck, back fast.
</Callout>
