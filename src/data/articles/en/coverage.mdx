The second of the three calls from the bench is **“Zuordnung!”** (coverage, literally
“assignment”). It is about our own zone, the third of the ice in front of our goal. There,
each of the five skaters has their own area. This article explains where those areas are
and what to do in them.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
Our zone is split into **five areas**: the **defenders** each have one side low down, the
**wingers** each one side higher up, the **centre** starts in the middle in front of the
goal. If the puck is in your area, you put on **pressure**. The **centre** helps as near
support, and the **far-side defender** goes to the front of the goal. A player who comes
into the zone does **not skate to the puck**, but looks: **which area is still free?**
That is where they go.
</Callout>

## What “Zuordnung” means

Children like to skate to the puck all together. In hockey this is sometimes called a
**beehive**. Then four players stand in one corner and nobody is in front of the goal. A
single pass is enough for the opponent to score.

That is why the team spreads out in our own zone. Everyone takes an area. If an opponent
comes into your area, they are yours. If they move on to another area, you hand them over
to the teammate there. This way of defending is called **zone defence**: you cover a space,
not a particular opponent.

When someone calls **“Zuordnung!”**, it means: look where you belong and take your area.
The call usually comes when too many players are at the puck or an area is empty.

## The five areas

Our zone is divided up like a house with five rooms. The border between low and high runs
just above the faceoff dots:

| Area | Where | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low left** | left corner and left faceoff circle, up to just above the dot | LD |
| **Low right** | right corner and right faceoff circle, up to just above the dot | RD |
| **Middle** | a strip as wide as the crease, from the goal into the slot | C (starting point) |
| **High left** | from the boards to the middle, up to the blue line | LW |
| **High right** | from the boards to the middle, up to the blue line | RW |

The **slot** is the space right in front of the goal, where most goals come from. Higher
up, between the two wingers, the middle strip continues to the blue line. The centre and
the wingers share that part.

For defenders and wingers the same applies in every area:

- **If the puck is in your area**, you put on pressure, on the
  <a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a>.
- **If an attacker without the puck is in your area**, you are between them and the goal.
- **If the puck is on the other side**, you move to the edge of your area, towards the puck
  and the middle. The far-side defender is then in front of the goal.

### The centre: support first

The centre has a special role. The middle is their starting point, but they don’t stand
still there. They are the **near support** at the puck: if a defender is battling for the
puck in the corner, the centre goes down low and helps. More in the article
<a href={href('/en/support')}>Support</a>.

There is one exception: if **an attacker in front of the goal is open** and the far-side
defender can’t cover them, the centre goes there. An open attacker in front of the goal is
more dangerous than a battle in the corner.

When the puck moves, everyone shifts with it. Try it: tap a spot and watch who goes where.

## The area counts, not the person

In a game the right player isn’t always in the right place. The centre might be battling
in the corner, with the defender helping from the side. Then they **swap**. The
centre plays defender until the puck is out of the zone.

What doesn’t help: skating across the ice to your “own” position while the opponent uses
the gap. Swap back when things are calm – when the puck has left the zone or at a
stoppage.

That is why every child at U13 learns every position (more in the article
<a href={href('/en/lineup')}>The lineup</a>). If you know what a defender does in front of
the goal, you can take over that job as a forward too.

## Finding the open gap

Coverage matters most when a player **comes into the zone**: after a line change, after
skating back, or after losing a battle. Then:

1. **Head up.** Before you skate into the zone, look across the ice.
2. **Count.** Who is at the puck? Is anyone in front of the goal? Is the middle closed?
3. **Skate into the free area.** If more than one is free, the front of the goal comes
   first, then the slot. That is where it is most dangerous.
4. **Say what you are doing.** A short “I’ve got the front!” helps the others.

A fourth player at the puck hardly ever helps. But they are always missing somewhere else.
If two are already at the puck, your place is almost certainly somewhere else.

## Common mistakes

- **Everyone to the puck.** The beehive. Nobody is in front of the goal.
- **Following your opponent everywhere.** Then your area is empty and the next opponent
  skates into it.
- **Leaving too early.** The wingers head for the blue line before we have the puck under
  control. Win the puck first, then go.
- **Standing and watching.** An area is not a parking space. Turn your head, stick on the
  ice, keep the opponents in view.
- **Two players behind the goal.** Nobody can shoot from behind the goal. One is enough
  there; the others belong in front of it.

## Coverage and the other calls

The three calls belong together. In your area, the
<a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a> always applies: between the
opponent and the goal. And the two players at the puck – pressure and support – are
already a form of <a href={href('/en/support')}>support</a>.
