Ice hockey looks wild. But almost everything that happens on the ice comes down to one
simple question: **who has time and space?**

**Time** means: how long until an opponent reaches you? **Space** means: how much free ice
is around you? With both, you can look, pass and shoot calmly. Without them, you make
mistakes.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
Picture a **bubble** around the player with the puck. It is as big as the free ice around
them.

- **We have the puck:** make the bubble **bigger**. Look beforehand for where the room is,
  skate into open ice, pass to a free teammate.
- **The opponents have the puck:** make their bubble **smaller**. Close the gap, cover the
  passing lanes, push them to the boards.
</Callout>

## What time and space mean

Time and space belong together. If the nearest opponent is far away, you have lots of
room. And because it takes them a long time to reach you, you also have lots of time. If
they are close, you have little of either.

A **rule of thumb** helps you estimate it: an opponent at speed covers about **5 metres a
second**. At 10 metres away you have roughly 2 seconds. At 5 metres, about one second is
left. That sounds short. But one second is enough for a good pass.

In the drawings on this page, the bubble is a **purple circle** around the player with the
puck. The number next to it says roughly how many seconds they have.

## Gaining time and space

When our team has the puck, the player with the puck is the
<a href={href('/en/roles')}>König</a> (king), K in the drawings. They and all their
teammates want the same thing: a big bubble around the puck. There are several ways to get it.

### Spotting open ice

There is almost always **open ice** – you just have to see it. Good spots are where **no
opponent is responsible**. These are often called *soft areas*: soft spots in the
defence. Three are typical:

- **In the corner on the puck side.** The opponent attacking the König stands between them
  and the goal. Behind that opponent the corner is often empty. The puck gets there off the
  boards: with a bank pass or a *rim*, a pass that slides along the boards. Behind the
  net, the teammate from the far post picks it up.
- **High in the slot**, in front of the goal between the faceoff circles. When the opponents
  stand deep in front of their goal, there is often nobody there.
- **At the faceoff circle, next to the passing shadow.** The **passing shadow** is the space
  behind an opponent that no pass gets through to. One or two steps to the side and you are
  free.

Skate there without the puck and the pass gives you a big bubble straight away. Stand right
next to an opponent and you are no help to the puck carrier.

<RinkPlay play="soft-areas" locale="en" />

With the puck it works the same way: away from the opponent, into open ice. The opponent
has to chase you, and that costs them time. But you only see open ice if you look over your
shoulder beforehand. That is called **scanning** – more on it under
<a href={href('/en/roles')}>König</a>.

### The pass is faster

A pass is two to three times as fast as a player on skates. That makes the pass the
quickest way to more time: the bubble travels with the puck. If your bubble is small and a
free teammate’s is big, play the puck to them.

<Callout type="tipp" title="The fastest teammate">
Who is the fastest teammate that never gets tired? **The puck.** Let it do the work.
</Callout>

For that, the teammate without the puck, the Satellit (satellite, S), needs a free spot where they can
take a pass. That is the idea behind <a href={href('/en/support')}>support</a> and
<a href={href('/en/skating-to-the-puck')}>skating to the puck</a>.

### Making space without the puck

A player without the puck can win time for the König too. They skate to where an opponent
has to react. If the opponent follows, they are missing at the König. If they stay, our
player is free.

A classic: right after entering the attacking zone, the teammate without the puck skates
**between the two defenders** straight to the net – known as splitting the D. While the
defenders decide who takes them, the König’s bubble grows.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="split-d" locale="en" />

### Slowing down: time for the teammates

Gaining time doesn’t always mean being fast. Sometimes the König is alone up front and the
teammates are still far behind. Skate alone into three opponents and the puck is gone.

Better: **slow the play down.** The König turns away on the boards, keeps the puck and faces
the play again. This is called a *delay*. The seconds gained this way belong to the whole
team: the teammates catch up and find open ice. Then comes the pass.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="delay" locale="en" />

## Taking time and space away

When the opponents have the puck, it is the other way round. Now **their** bubble should
shrink. An opponent without time has to guess. They pass badly or lose the puck.

### Closing the gap

The **gap** is the distance between you and the opponent with the puck. The bigger it is,
the more time they have. The player defending the puck carrier is called the
<a href={href('/en/roles')}>Jäger</a> (hunter, J) in our team. They close the gap with a
few quick strides.

They stay on the <a href={href('/en/defensive-side')}>defensive side</a>, between the
opponent and the goal. Just before reaching the opponent, they slow down. That way the
opponent can’t simply skate past. All that is left for the opponent is the way wide, to the
**boards**. The boards are like a wall: on that side they have no room at all.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="bubble-shrink" locale="en" />

### Covering the passing lanes

An opponent with a small bubble looks for a way out: a pass to a free teammate. That way
can be closed. That is the job of the <a href={href('/en/roles')}>Wächter</a> (guard, W):
they hold their stick in the **passing lane** – where the pass would have to go through.
Behind it lies the passing shadow. No pass gets there, and the opponent loses their last
option.

So even a player who never touches the puck helps. A teammate with a big bubble is no use
to the opponents if no pass can get through to them.

<RinkSequence client:visible sequence="close-lane" locale="en" />

Both together are the idea behind the <a href={href('/en/forecheck')}>forecheck</a>: one
player herds the opponent towards the boards, the others cover the passing lanes.

## Common mistakes

- **Standing still with the puck.** Standing still gives the opponent time to come closer.
  The bubble shrinks by itself.
- **Hitting the puck away blindly.** That gains no time. Usually the opponents then have
  the puck – and a big bubble.
- **Everyone skates to the puck.** Then the ice everywhere else is free. One pass, and the
  opponent has a huge bubble. That is why one attacks, one helps, and the others stay in
  their <a href={href('/en/coverage')}>area</a>.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents in the stands">
At the next game, watch the player with the puck and ask yourself: how big is their bubble
right now? Is it growing or shrinking? You will see: good plays make the bubble bigger.
Good defence makes it smaller. That holds even when nobody scores.
</Callout>
