In the dressing room you hear words like "gino", "apple" or "bar down". If you don't know
them, you only understand half of what is said. This article explains the most important
words, the unwritten rules on the ice and a few stories every hockey fan knows.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
A **gino** is a goal, an **apple** an assist, a **biscuit** the puck. A **hat trick** is
three goals in one game. **Gretzky's office** is the spot behind the opponents' net.
And the most important unwritten rule: **after the whistle, play is over**, and you never
embarrass the opponents.
</Callout>

## Why hockey has its own language

Ice hockey comes from Canada. That is why most of its words are English, in Germany too.
German players use many of them just as they are, like flex or lie for sticks. Here they
are explained.

A few words are very old. **Puck** probably comes from the Irish word "poc", a strike. The
German word for a faceoff, **Bully**, goes back to the early days of the game: both
players struck the ice with their sticks three times, then each other's stick three
times, and only then went for the puck.

Many of these words are slang, casual everyday language. You hear them in the dressing
room, on YouTube and on NHL broadcasts. The NHL is the best professional league in the
world, in North America.

## Goals, assists and the puck

A **gino** or **tuck** is a goal. "He scored a gino" means: he scored.

An **apple** is an assist, the last or second-to-last pass before a goal.

A **biscuit** is the puck. "Biscuit in the basket" means: the puck is in the net.

A **celly** is a goal celebration.

A **snipe** is an accurate, hard shot that goes in exactly where it was meant to. Someone
who scores like that a lot is a **sniper**.

To **dangle** or **deke** means to beat an opponent with the puck. A deke is a fake, for
example showing left and then going past on the right.

A **tape-to-tape** pass is a perfect pass. The puck goes from the tape on one stick straight
onto the tape of a teammate's. Tape is the sticky tape round the blade.

**Sauce** is a pass that flies over the opponent's stick and lands flat on the ice like a
saucer. Its full name is "saucer pass".

An **empty netter** is a goal into an empty net, usually near the end, when the opponents
have taken their goalie off for an extra skater.

The spots in the net have their own words too. **Top shelf** is the highest shelf. There
is a saying for it: "where Mama hides the cookies".

## Hat tricks

A **hat trick** is three goals by one player in one game. The word comes from cricket. In
1858 a bowler there took three wickets with three balls in a row. The spectators collected
money and bought him a hat.

In ice hockey it became a custom. The best-known story comes from Toronto: in the 1940s a
hatter named Sammy Taft gave a hat to every player who scored three goals there. Similar
stories are told about Montreal and Guelph. Today fans throw their caps and hats onto the
ice after a hat trick. In the NHL the scorer usually gets first pick of them. What is left
is often donated.

A **natural hat trick** is stricter: three goals in a row, with no other goal in between,
not even one by the opponents.

A **Gordie Howe hat trick** means a goal, an assist and a fight in the same game. Gordie
Howe was one of the greatest players in history, famous for his skill and for his
toughness. He himself managed this "hat trick" only twice.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Fights are not part of the game">
Under the rules played in Germany too, a fight means a five-minute penalty and being sent
off for the rest of the game. A suspension often follows. The Gordie Howe hat trick is a
story from another time, not a goal to aim for.
</Callout>

## Places on the ice

**Gretzky's office** is the spot behind the opponents' net. Wayne Gretzky, the player
with the most points in NHL history, had the puck there so often it was like his place of
work. From behind the net you can see the whole ice. The opponents, though, have to turn
round and can't see the puck and their player at the same time.

<RinkPlay play="gretzky-office" locale="en" />

Behind the net is also where the craziest trick in ice hockey starts: the **Michigan**,
also called the **lacrosse goal**. The player scoops the puck up onto the blade behind the
net, holds it there, skates round the net and throws it into the top corner. Just like in
lacrosse, where you carry the ball in a net on your stick.

The name comes from Mike Legg, a student at the University of Michigan. In 1996 he scored
the first goal like this in an important game. In the NHL it only happened for the first
time in 2019, by Andrei Svechnikov. The Michigan is allowed as long as the stick isn't
higher than the crossbar when the puck goes in. You can practise it at any training
session, without a goalie at first.

The **slot** is the area right in front of the net, between the faceoff dots. Most goals
are scored from there.

The **crease** is the blue goal area. In there the goalie has right of way.

The **sin bin** is the penalty box (Strafbank).

The **barn** is the rink. And **the room** is the dressing room (Kabine).

The **Zamboni** is the ice resurfacer (Eismaschine) that smooths the ice before the game and
in the breaks. It shaves off the top layer and spreads warm water, which freezes into
smooth ice. The name comes from Frank Zamboni from California, who built the first such
machine in 1949. Today lots of people call any ice resurfacer a "Zamboni", no matter who
made it.

## Player types

A **playmaker** makes lots of assists. They see the open teammate before anyone else
notices.

A **grinder** works hard, wins the battles along the boards and never gives up. No team
wins without grinders.

A **two-way player** is just as good at both ends, in attack and in defence.

An **enforcer**, also called a **goon**, used to be a player picked mainly for fighting. The
job was to protect the team's best players. In the NHL this role has almost disappeared:
around 1984 there was more than one fight per game, in 2019 fewer than one in five games.
In youth hockey it has no place. Today you protect teammates differently: with support,
so that nobody is left alone with the puck.

Some words are insults. **Pigeon** is a put-down for a player who "picks up crumbs" in
front of the net, scoring rebounds that others worked for. Yet that is exactly good
hockey: whoever stands in front of the net and follows up scores lots of goals. **Sieve**
is an insult for a goalie who lets in many goals. A **duster** is a player who gets on the
ice so rarely that their gear gathers dust on the bench. That isn't meant kindly either.

<Callout type="merke" title="Leave those words outside">
It's worth knowing pigeon, sieve, duster and similar words, so you understand them. They have no
place in your own dressing room, not even as a joke.
</Callout>

## In practice

A **line skate** (Linienlauf) goes like this: from the goal line to the next line and
back, then to the line after that and back, all the way to the far goal line. Each time as
fast as you can, with a hard stop at every line.

A **bag skate** is an especially long, hard line skate, until everyone is completely worn
out. "Bagged" is slang for exhausted. In North America the bag skate is known as a
punishment, when a team has played or practised badly.

Line skates are also perfectly normal training for speed and fitness, though. If you can
still stop cleanly at the end of a line skate, you have the strength in a game for the
last battle for the puck.

## The Code: unwritten rules

Ice hockey has rules that aren't in any rule book. They are called "the Code". Almost all
of them are about respect.

**After the whistle, play is over.** Once the referee blows the whistle, nobody shoots at
the net any more. A goal wouldn't count anyway, and every shot after the whistle is
dangerous and disrespectful to the goalie. The referee can give a penalty for it.

**Leave the goalie alone.** Nobody pushes or crowds the goalie in the crease. After the
whistle, nobody pokes at a puck the goalie has covered.

**We protect our goalie.** That is the other side of the same rule: nobody touches our
goalie. If an opponent crashes into them or keeps poking after the whistle, one of us is
there straight away. Protecting means:

- calmly standing between the opponent and the goalie, without pushing,
- clearing the puck out of the crease before an opponent can follow up,
- taking away the opponent's space in front of the net, with your body and with your stick
  on the ice,
- and if something wasn't right: the captain talks to the referee.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Protecting doesn't mean hitting back">
Whoever pushes back or throws a punch gets the penalty. The referee nearly always sees the
second action, not the first. Then you are sitting in the penalty box, and the team and the
goalie are a player short. Your hands stay on your stick. You protect the goalie with your
body and by staying calm, not with your fists.
</Callout>

**Never embarrass the opponents.** A team far in the lead keeps playing normally. It
doesn't show off with the puck and doesn't celebrate every goal like a final. Playing badly
on purpose would be disrespectful too, though.

**Chirping** means trash talk, cheeky remarks at the opponents. In the NHL it is part of
the game. It's better to leave it. Anyone who does it anyway never makes remarks about a
person, their looks, their family or where they come from.

**In the warm-up, everyone stays in their own half.** Before the game both teams warm up
at the same time, each in its own half. The red centre line is an invisible wall: nobody
skates across it, and nobody shoots a puck into the other half, let alone at the other
net. In some leagues that counts as a challenge, and it has led to fights before the game
had even started.

**The handshake.** After the game both teams line up and shake hands. That goes for a win
just as much as for a loss. The custom is more than a hundred years old.

**Stick taps.** Tapping your stick on the ice or the boards is the players' applause. You
tap for a good play and for an injured player who gets back up. Even when they play for
the other team.

**The puck from your first goal.** In the NHL every player gets the puck from their first
goal. In U13 that isn't possible: the game puck stays in the game. Sometimes, though, there
is another puck as a keepsake after the game. The custom: you wrap white tape round the
puck and write on it what happened, for example "First goal", the date and the opponents.
At FASS U13 we try to do that whenever we can.

## Superstitions

Lots of players are superstitious. A few examples from the NHL:

**Playoff beards.** In the playoffs, the games for the title, many players stop shaving
until their team is knocked out or wins. The custom started with the New York Islanders in
the early 1980s.

**Don't touch the wrong trophy.** The team that wins the semi-final gets a trophy. Some
captains won't touch it, because the only trophy they want to lift is the Stanley Cup.
Others touch it and win anyway.

**Talking to the net.** The goalie Patrick Roy talked to his goalposts during games. He
said they sometimes answered: "Ping".

**Don't step on the logo.** In many dressing rooms the team logo is printed on the floor.
Stepping on it shows no respect.

**Who leaves the ice last?** In some leagues a player, often the goalie, insists on being
the last one off the ice after the warm-up. Sometimes players from both teams then wait at
the door, and nobody wants to go first. It looks odd, and the Zamboni is waiting. In 1987
a ritual like this ended in a huge brawl in the NHL: two Montreal players shot a puck into
the opponents' empty net after the warm-up, as they always did. The Philadelphia players
had warned them. Afterwards the NHL brought in much tougher penalties.

On top of that come everyone's own rituals: always putting on the left skate first, taping
the stick the same way, eating the same thing before a game.

## Stories worth knowing

**The Stanley Cup.** Lord Stanley, then Governor General of Canada, bought a silver bowl in
1892 for 10 guineas. It was first awarded in 1893. Today it is the most famous trophy in
ice hockey. The winners' names are engraved on it, with a few typos: "Bqstqn Bruins"
instead of "Boston Bruins", "Maple Leaes" instead of "Maple Leafs". Since 1995 every player
on the winning team may take the Cup home for a day.

**The first mask.** Goalies used to play without masks. On 1 November 1959 a shot hit
Jacques Plante in the face. He came back onto the ice wearing a home-made mask and wore it
from then on. He wasn't the first with a mask, but he was the first to wear one every game.
Today everyone in youth hockey wears a helmet with a cage, skaters and goalies alike.

**Number 99.** As a kid, Wayne Gretzky wore 9, the number of his hero Gordie Howe. At 16
he joined a junior team where another player already had 9. His coach suggested: then
take two nines. That is how the most famous number in ice hockey came about.

Gretzky became the player with the most assists and the most points in NHL history. So the league
honoured him in a way it never had before: in 2000, number 99 was taken out of use for
every team. When a team stops giving out a number, people say the number is "retired".
Normally each team does that on its own, for its own legends. 99 is the only number
retired across the whole NHL. Nobody there will ever wear it again.

**The octopus.** In 1952 two brothers in Detroit threw an octopus onto the ice. Its eight
arms stood for the eight wins it took to win the Stanley Cup back then. Detroit won all
eight. Octopuses still land on the ice in Detroit today.

**Teddy bears.** At a "teddy bear toss" the fans throw cuddly toys onto the ice after their
team's first goal. The toys are given to children. The record belongs to Hershey in the
USA, with more than 102,000 cuddly toys in 2025. There are events like this in Germany too.

**The Miracle on Ice.** At the 1980 Olympics in Lake Placid, a team of American college
students beat the Soviet Union 4–3. The Soviet Union was thought to be unbeatable. The game
wasn't even the final, but it is still the most famous game in Olympic history.

**Germany wins silver.** In 2018 Germany reached the Olympic final in Pyeongchang and only
lost 3–4 in overtime. In 2023 Germany won silver at the World Championship, its first World
Championship medal since 1953.
