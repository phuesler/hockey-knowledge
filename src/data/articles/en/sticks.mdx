There are fifty sticks on the rack in the shop. They all look the same, and the shafts are
covered in numbers like **P28** or **Flex 50**. This article explains what those numbers
mean and which of them actually make a difference for a U13 player.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
Only three things have to be right: **length**, **flex** and **shooting side**. Everything
else – curve, lie, material – is fine-tuning. A stick that is too long or too stiff holds
your child's technique back more than any curve ever could.
</Callout>

## Why the stick matters at all

After the skates, the stick is the piece of equipment that matters most: it's part of every
pass, every shot and every time your child receives the puck. If it is too long, your child
stands too upright and loses balance and speed. If it is too stiff, they can't bend it when
they shoot – and that bend is exactly what sends the puck flying. A stick that is far too
soft, on the other hand, breaks more easily and makes shots inaccurate.

Kids grow fast at this age. So plan for a stick that fits now, not one they are supposed to
grow into.

## Length: up to the nose

The way to measure is simple and works in any shop:

1. Have your child stand up straight **without skates**, in trainers.
2. Stand the stick upright in front of them, with the toe of the blade on the floor.
3. The top end should reach about **up to the nose**.

On skates you are about five centimetres taller – there the same stick reaches **up to the
chin**. Both rules mean the same length, just measured differently.

As a number: the stick length is roughly **body height minus 12 centimetres**.

A few centimetres either way are normal. Players who want lots of puck control and quick
hands go for the shorter end; players who need reach in defence go for the longer end. For
U13: better a little too short than too long.

## Flex: go a bit softer

The **flex** describes how easily the shaft bends. The number on the stick is the force in
pounds it takes to bend it by one inch. Small number = soft stick.

The old rule of thumb was: **flex ≈ body weight in kg**. So a child weighing 40 kg would need
a flex of about 40.

We recommend going a bit softer: body weight in kg minus 8. So a child weighing 40 kg needs a
flex of about 32. If needed, add an extension (Verlängerung) instead.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Cutting and extending change the stick">
Every centimetre you saw off the top makes the stick stiffer – as a rule of thumb about
**5 flex points per 2.5 cm**. So if you buy a stick that has to be cut down by 10 cm, you
end up with a stick that feels like flex 70 instead of 50. An extension works the other way
round: the stick feels softer.

On top of that, the manufacturer tunes the stick for its full length. Cutting or extending
a lot also moves the spot where the stick bends – the **kick point** (more on that in the
next section).
</Callout>

So the rule is: **change as little as possible.** Compare different lengths and models in
the shop. Take the stick that needs the least cutting or extending.

An example: your child needs 140 cm. Stick A is 147 cm long and would have to be cut down by
7 cm. Stick B is 136 cm long and needs a 4 cm extension. Then stick B is the better choice.

## Kick point: where the stick bends

The **kick point** is the spot on the shaft where the stick bends most when you shoot. The
flex tells you **how much** a stick bends, the kick point tells you **where**. Every stick
has one, even if it isn't always printed on the label.

<KickPoints />

**Mid kick.** The bend sits in the middle of the shaft, roughly where the lower hand is. The
stick takes a little longer to load, but then releases a lot of energy. This is the classic
stick for wrist shots and slapshots.

**Low kick.** The bend sits just above the blade. The stick loads very quickly and is built
for the snapshot – the short shot from the wrists, without a backswing. That only works if
the technique is right: a player who still pushes the puck from back to front, instead of
pressing into it briefly, gets little out of a low kick.

**Hybrid.** The bending zone is longer and sits between mid and low. The stick gives a bit
with almost any shooting technique, which makes it the most forgiving.

The kick point on the label applies to the stick as it comes out of the shop. Cut down or
extended a lot, it bends somewhere other than intended. A mid kick then isn't quite what you
paid for any more.

<Callout type="tipp" title="Our recommendation for U13">
**Hybrid or mid kick**, as long as the snapshot isn't there yet. A low-kick stick rewards a
technique that most kids this age don't have yet – and until then it just feels like it has
no pop. Once your child snaps the puck cleanly, switching to a low kick is a decision worth
making on purpose.
</Callout>

Almost every big manufacturer has at least one product line for each kick point. If you know
which kick point you're looking for, this overview helps in the shop. It is not a buying
recommendation.

| Manufacturer | Mid kick | Hybrid | Low kick |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CCM | Tacks | Jetspeed | Trigger |
| Warrior | Novium | Alpha LX | Covert |
| Bauer | Nexus | Pulse (Bauer calls it "Fusion") | Vapor (e.g. Flylite, Hyperlite) |
| Sherwood | True Touch | Code | Rekker |

The model names within a line change almost every year. The line itself and its kick point
usually stay the same.

## The size classes

Manufacturers make sticks in four sizes. They differ not only in length but also in shaft
thickness and blade size – a senior stick feels wrong in a child's hands even after it has
been cut down.

On the shaft the differences are only a few millimetres. A senior shaft is about 3 mm wider
and 2 mm thicker than a junior shaft. That sounds like nothing. A child's hand feels it,
though: it can't grip the thicker shaft as firmly, and that costs control when stickhandling
and shooting.

<ShaftSizes />

The measurements are guide values, measured by hand. How round the corners are and what shape
the shaft has exactly varies from model to model:

<ShaftShapes />

There is no better shape. It's a matter of taste. Let your child hold several sticks in the
shop and take the one that feels good.

| Size | Body height | Typical flex | Age (roughly) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Youth (Yth) | up to approx. 140 cm | 20–35 | up to 8 |
| Junior (Jr) | approx. 140–160 cm | 30–50 | 8–13 |
| Intermediate (Int) | approx. 155–175 cm | 50–65 | 12–15 |
| Senior (Sr) | from approx. 170 cm | 65–85 | 14 and up |

Most U13 players end up with **junior**, the tallest ones already with **intermediate**.

## Left or right?

A stick is made either for a left shot or a right shot (Linksschütze / Rechtsschütze), and
that has nothing to do with the hand your child writes with. The **stronger hand goes on
top**, at the knob – it guides the stick. That's why right-handers surprisingly often shoot
left.

Here's how to find out: give your child a broom and let them sweep without thinking about
it. The hand on top shows the side. If the left hand is low on the shaft, they are a **left
shot**.

Don't switch later. The side that feels natural at 11 is the right one.

## Curve, lie and all the rest

These details are printed on every stick and are **secondary** for U13. Still, here is a
short explanation so you're not left puzzled in the shop:

**The curve** describes how the blade (Kelle) is bent:

- **Shallow mid curve** – the curve sits in the middle and is slight, the face only slightly
  open. Good for passing and hard, low shots (P88).
- **Mid curve** – the curve sits in the middle, slightly towards the toe. The all-rounder,
  and the right choice if you're unsure (P92).
- **Toe curve** – the curve sits out at the toe. For players who work a lot with the toe and
  lift pucks quickly (P28).

<BladeCurves />

Every manufacturer gives the same curve its own name. We use Bauer's names. This table helps
when the stick says something else:

| Bauer | CCM | Warrior | Sherwood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P92 | P29 | W03 | PP92 |
| P28 | P28 | W28 | PP28 |
| P88 | P88 | W88 | PP88 |

If there is an **M** after the name (P92M, MX at Sherwood, sometimes also "Max"), the blade
is a little taller: the same curve, but more material along the top edge. That helps in
battles along the boards, on tip-ins and in faceoffs. We haven't seen it on junior sticks yet,
but we have on intermediate ones.

Then there is the **face**: an open blade lifts the puck more easily, a closed one keeps it
lower on the ice. For a child who is still working on puck control, a **mid curve with a
moderately open face** is the simplest choice.

<Callout type="tipp" title="Our recommendation for U13">
**P92** (CCM calls it P29). The curve sits in the middle and the face is open, but not
extreme. That makes stickhandling, passing and shooting work equally well, and almost every
shop has it in stock. Players who already work a lot with the toe and like to lift the puck
quickly can try **P28**.
</Callout>

**The lie** is the angle between shaft and blade, given as 5, 5.5 or 6. It is right when the
blade **lies flat on the ice** in a normal skating stance. If the toe is up, the lie is too
high; if only the toe touches, it is too low. After practice, look at the underside of the
blade: the wear shows where the stick really touches the ice. Lie 5 is the usual value for
most youth sticks.

<LieAngles />

Length and lie are connected. A player with a very long or a very short stick holds it at a
different angle. To keep as much of the blade on the ice as possible, some players then pick
a different lie. That's a topic for professionals. For U13: length by the nose rule, lie 5,
done.

## Wood or composite?

<Callout type="tipp" title="Last season's model or second-hand">
A stick from last season is usually just as good as the new one, but costs a lot less in a
**clearance sale** (Abverkauf). Buying **second-hand** is worth it too: kids grow out of
their sticks quickly. So you can often get a very good stick that has only been played for
one season, at a good price. Look it over carefully first – how to spot a broken stick is
explained <a href="#when-a-stick-is-broken">further down</a>. And ask whether it has been
cut down: then it is stiffer than the flex printed on the shaft.
</Callout>

Wooden sticks are now almost only made for the youngest players. Everything from junior up
is practically always **composite**, meaning carbon and fibreglass. The prices below are for
junior sticks:

- **Entry-level composite (approx. 60–100 euros)** – a bit heavier, but tough. Good enough for
  U13.
- **Mid-range (approx. 110–170 euros)** – lighter, with a better feel when shooting. The
  difference from the entry-level model is clearly noticeable. Not a must, but a real help.
- **Top models (approx. 190–230 euros)** – very light, very thin-walled, and that's exactly
  why they are fragile. Money thrown away at this age.

Every size up costs more. A top model costs around 170 to 200 euros as a youth stick, 190 to
230 euros as a junior, 230 to 280 euros as an intermediate and 280 to 320 euros as a senior.
So in junior you can still get top models for under 200 euros. In intermediate that gets
difficult: under 200 euros you'll find only the odd model there, often last year's.

<Callout type="tipp" title="A second stick">
Sticks break, usually at the worst possible moment. A second stick of the same length in the
bag saves you a game with borrowed gear. A cheap spare stick is good enough for that.
</Callout>

## What the pros really play

The pros' sticks usually show the brand's newest model. What they actually play is often
something else: a stick the manufacturer builds to the player's wishes, with their own curve,
flex and kick point. The graphics on it advertise the model on the rack in the shop. Some pros
even play a model under the new paint job that hasn't been on sale for years.

Don't let that impress you. The stick your child sees on TV is not the one on the rack in
the shop.

On top of that, the most expensive models are built for **performance**, not for
**durability**. They are lighter and thinner-walled and so break sooner. Pros get their
sticks from the manufacturer in large numbers, so a broken stick is no problem for them. For
you it is.

<Callout type="merke" title="It's the craftsman, not the tools">
The best players play very well even with an average stick. A better stick still helps, up
to a point. The biggest jump is the switch from wood to composite. There is a second, clearly
noticeable difference between the entry-level and the mid-range model. Above that, every
extra euro brings less and less. What matters is that length and flex fit. Practice does the
rest.
</Callout>

## Smaller manufacturers

Besides the big brands there are smaller companies that sell their sticks directly online,
with no shop in between. Two examples:

**[Pro Stock Hockey Sticks](https://prostockhockeysticks.com/)** from Canada. The hybrid
model is called **VF** and is very good for its price. A junior costs around 90 to 120 US
dollars. The catch: shipping to Europe costs around 30 to 40 euros, plus import VAT and
possibly customs duty. Shipping is free from an order value of around 230 euros, which is
about three sticks. So it is mainly worth it when several families order together.

**[MyHockeyStick](https://myhockeystick.eu/en-eu)** from Slovakia. The sticks are called
**PHAZE**, the hybrid model is the **PHAZE Predict**. Junior costs 159 euros, intermediate
169 euros. Because the company is based in the EU there is no customs duty, and shipping is
free from two sticks. Junior only comes in flex 40, though – rather too stiff for light kids.

The downside of both: you can't hold the stick in your hands before you buy it.

## Tape

The knob at the top end is a must – without it the hand slides off the stick, and in many
leagues the referees check it. Make it yourself: wrap tape (Griffband) several times around
the end until you can feel a bump, then tape over it smoothly. At the very least, the end cap
should be secured with tape so that it doesn't fall out.

On the shaft, everything is a matter of taste. A spiral from top to bottom or separate rings
a few centimetres apart are both common. The blade gets stick tape: it protects the material,
calms the puck and makes receiving passes easier.

## When a stick is broken

A composite stick rarely snaps cleanly. Look out for these signs:

- A **dull sound** instead of the usual bright crack when shooting.
- A **white crack** or a dent in the carbon.
- The shaft feels **soft** in one spot or creaks when you bend it.

A cracked stick breaks on the next hard shot. Repairing it isn't worth it.

## Checklist for the shop

- [ ] Bring your child – without trying it out it's a matter of luck
- [ ] Shooting side found with the broom test
- [ ] Length checked: end of the stick at the nose, without skates
- [ ] Flex suits the weight, taking into account how much will be cut off
- [ ] Chose the stick that needs the least cutting or extending
- [ ] Right size class, not just the right length
- [ ] Kick point: hybrid or mid, as long as the snapshot isn't there yet
- [ ] Mid curve if you're unsure (P92/P29)
- [ ] Knob and tape planned in

<Callout type="merke" title="Rules of thumb, not laws">
All the numbers here are based on experience. If a stick feels good for your child and they
shoot cleanly with it, it's the right one – even if the table says otherwise.
</Callout>
