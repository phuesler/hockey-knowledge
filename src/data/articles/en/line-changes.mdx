One turn on the ice, a **shift**, lasts only 40 to 60 seconds. Then the legs are tired and
the next line comes on. How that works at the boards, who sits where and who works the door
is in the article on the <a href={href('/en/lineup')}>lineup</a>. This one is about the
other question: **when** do you change (Wechsel)?

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
- **Puck going away from our goal: you may go.** Puck coming towards our goal: you stay
  out.
- **Periods 1 and 3:** the bench is close to our goal. A short way from our own zone, a
  long way from the attack. Forwards change **on the way back**.
- **Period 2:** the bench is far from our goal, the **long change**. Defenders change
  **while we attack**.
- **Defenders:** when one changes, the other covers at the back.
- **Out too long?** Over the red line, puck deep into the corner, off to the bench.
</Callout>

## Why the moment matters

A change takes a few seconds. During that time someone is missing from the play: whoever
heads to the bench no longer helps, and whoever comes on is not yet where the play is. If
the opponents attack right then, they outnumber us in front of our goal.

Changing too late is bad too. Tired legs are slow, and tired heads make mistakes. So the
rule is: **short shifts, change at the right moment.**

## Where the bench is

Both benches are on the same boards, side by side, either side of the centre red line. In
period 1 our bench is in **our own half**, close to our goal.

After every period the teams switch ends. The benches stay where they are. So in period 2
our bench is in the **opponents' half**, far from our own goal. In period 3 it is like
period 1 again.

The drawings show the rink as you see it from the stands: the benches always stay in the
same place. So in period 2 **our goal is at the top**, and we attack downwards.

<RinkPlay play="bench-distance" locale="en" />

The long way in period 2 is called the **long change**. It makes period 2 harder than the
other two.

## Periods 1 and 3: the short change

**For defenders it is easy.** From our own zone the bench is close. Once the puck is out of
our zone, a defender skates the few metres to the bench. Whoever comes off the bench fresh
jumps on near our blue line and is in the play straight away.

**For forwards it is harder** when we have been pressing in the attacking zone for a while.
From there the bench is a long way off. Change in the middle of the attack and we are a
player short up front, and the replacement is a long way from joining in.

So for forwards in periods 1 and 3:

- **Keep your shift short.** Don't stay up front forever just because it is fun. Otherwise
  you are tired exactly when you have to skate back.
- **Change on the way back.** When the opponents win the puck, you skate towards our goal
  anyway. The bench is on the way.
- **But only if your replacement gets ahead of the puck.** Whoever jumps on has to land
  between the puck and our goal. Otherwise: skate back first, change later.

## Period 2: the long change

In period 2 it is the other way round.

**While we attack, changing is easy.** The defenders are on the opponents' blue line, and
the bench is right next to them. A defender can change in a few seconds, and the
replacement is back on the blue line straight away.

**With the puck in our half, changing is dangerous.** It is a long way from our goal to the
bench. The replacement only jumps on past the red line, far behind the play. If the
opponents are coming at our goal right then, we are a player short there.

<RinkPlay play="long-change" locale="en" />

So in period 2: **puck in our half, you stay.** First the puck has to get out, ideally over
the red line and deep into the opponents' zone. Then you change.

## Defenders: one changes, one covers

Defenders don't have to stay back all the time. They may join the attack, more on that in
the <a href={href('/en/lineup')}>lineup</a>. For changes, though, the rule is: **the two
change one after the other, never together.**

When one heads to the bench, **the other covers at the back** (sichert ab). That means
staying between the puck and our goal, even if they would like to join the attack right
now. Only once the replacement is on the ice may they change themselves or go forward
again.

If both head to the bench at once, for a few seconds nobody stands between the puck and our
goal. One long pass is enough, and the opponents skate in alone on our goalie.

<Callout type="tipp" title="The exception">
If the puck is **deep in the opponents' zone**, for example behind their goal, both may
change together. It takes long enough for the opponents to get from there to our goal.
</Callout>

## Dump and change

Sometimes we are stuck in our own zone for a long time. At last we have the puck, but
everyone is tired. There is no energy left for an attack. That is exactly when a simple
trick helps: **dump and change**, play the puck deep and change. It also helps when most of
the players on the ice have simply been out for a long time.

1. **Over the red line.** Get the puck over the centre red line with a pass or on your
   stick.
2. **Deep into the corner.** Shoot it into a corner of the opponents' zone, not at the
   goal. Their goalie should not be able to catch it.
3. **Off to the bench.** Whoever is tired skates hard to the bench straight away.

This works because the opponents first have to fetch the puck from behind their goal. By
the time they are back at our goal, the fresh line is on the ice. It works especially well
in period 2, because then the bench is right by the red line.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Why over the red line?">
If you shoot the puck from **our half** over the opponents' goal line, that is
<a href={href('/en/rules')}>icing</a>. Then there is a faceoff in front of our goal, and we
**are not allowed to change**. The tired line has to stay out, exactly the opposite of what
we wanted.
</Callout>

## When you change

- **Skate hard to the bench.** Whoever glides in slowly makes the time we are a player short
  longer.
- **Never take the puck with you.** Skate to the bench with the puck and you will usually
  lose it. Pass it or dump it deep first, then change.
- **Jump only just before the boards.** Whoever goes on waits until the player coming off
  is about 1.5 metres from the boards. Jump too early and it is a penalty: too many players
  on the ice.
- **Look where the puck is first.** Whoever comes on goes where the team needs help right
  now. If the opponents are coming at our goal, that means: back first.

## To remember

Five short sentences for the bench and the ice:

| Sentence | Means |
| --- | --- |
| **“Puck goes away, you may go.”** | Change when the puck is moving away from our goal. |
| **“Puck comes at us, you stay.”** | Opponents coming at our goal: stay out. |
| **“One changes, one covers.”** | Defenders change one after the other. Whoever doesn't change covers at the back. |
| **“Red line, corner, bench.”** | Over the red line, deep into the corner, change. |
| **“Period 2, long way.”** | In period 2, change while we attack. |

## Common mistakes

- **Changing while the opponents come at us.** When you are tired you want the bench. But if
  the puck is heading for our goal, that is exactly when we are a player short.
- **Both defenders at once.** Then nobody is back.
- **Simply shooting the puck far up the ice.** From our half that is icing, and the tired
  line has to stay out.
- **Staying out too long.** A good attack is no reason to stay on the ice for two minutes.
  The next line is waiting, and your legs need a rest.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents in the stands">
In period 2, watch when players change. If someone changes while the puck is in front of
their own goal, it often gets dangerous. A deep puck into the corner followed by a quick
change looks dull, but it is often exactly the right decision.
</Callout>
