Rinks are loud, and the officials are often far away. So they talk with their hands. If
you know the signals, you know straight away what is going on: offside or a penalty? Goal
or no goal? This article shows every signal in the rule book, each with a small figure. On
a phone the figure moves as soon as you scroll to it.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
**Two kinds of officials:** the **referees** (Schiedsrichter) wear orange armbands. They
call penalties and decide on goals. The **linespersons** (Linienrichter) wear no
armbands. They call offside and icing.
**One arm up** means one of three things: delayed offside, possible icing or a delayed
penalty. Look at who raises the arm and where the puck is.
**For a penalty** the arm comes first, then the whistle, then the signal for the foul.
</Callout>

## Referee or linesperson?

Everyone who blows a whistle on the ice is an **official**. There are two kinds, and you
tell them apart by their arms:

- **Referees** wear an **orange armband** on each upper arm. They call the penalties and
  decide whether a goal counts.
- **Linespersons** wear **no armbands**. They call offside and icing and take almost all
  the face-offs.

At big games there are four officials on the ice: two referees and two linespersons. In
youth hockey there are often fewer, sometimes only two. Then both do everything, offside,
icing and penalties, and usually both wear armbands. The armband trick does not help
then. The situation does: where is the puck right now, and what happened just before?

**In the rule book:** <a href="https://blob.iihf.com/iihf-media/iihfmvc/media/downloads/rule%20book/2026-27_iihf_rule_book.pdf#page=63">Rule 31, Referees</a>, point 31.4: offside and icing are the linespersons’ job.

## Arm up: penalty, offside or icing?

Three signals look almost the same: one arm straight up. The difference is **who** raises
it and **what is happening**:

- **Linesperson, the puck has just come into the zone:** delayed offside. All attackers
  out of the zone, then the arm comes down and play goes on.
- **Linesperson, the puck is sliding towards the goal line:** possible icing. The arm
  stays up until icing is called or washed out.
- **Referee:** delayed penalty against the team that does not have the puck. Play goes on
  until that team touches the puck. That is why our goalie skates to the bench when the
  opponents have fouled.

## Signals during play

These signals come up in every game. For offside the whistle comes first, then the arm.
The **wash-out** (Auswinken) always means: no, that was nothing. From a linesperson: no
offside, no icing. From a referee: no goal, no hand pass.

## How a penalty is called

A penalty always happens in the same order:

1. **Arm up.** The referee has seen the foul. Play goes on as long as the team that
   fouled does not have the puck.
2. **Whistle.** As soon as that team touches the puck, the referee blows the whistle. If
   the team that fouled already has the puck, the whistle comes straight away.
3. **The signal.** The referee shows with their hands which foul it was. Those signals
   are further down.
4. **To the penalty box.** The referee points at the player and at the penalty box
   (Strafbank), and reports the penalty at the timekeeper’s table.

For you that means: skate straight to the penalty box, no arguing. Only the captain talks
to the referee. How long a penalty lasts and what being short-handed means is under
<a href={href('/en/rules/#penalties')}>Penalties</a>.

**In the rule book:** <a href="https://blob.iihf.com/iihf-media/iihfmvc/media/downloads/rule%20book/2026-27_iihf_rule_book.pdf#page=46">Rule 15, Calling of penalties</a>.

## Fouls with the body

These penalties are for hitting someone with the body where you must not: into the
boards, from behind, in the head or at the knee. Or for holding and getting in the way
instead of playing the puck.

## Fouls with the stick

The stick belongs on the puck, not on an opponent. Almost every way of using it wrongly
has its own signal.

## Penalties for the team

Some penalties are not for one player’s foul but for the whole team: too many players on
the ice, or wasting time. A player still has to sit them out. The time-out is not a
penalty, but it has a signal too.
