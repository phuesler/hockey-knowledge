Everything depends on the skate: how a child stands, how they take corners, how quickly they
get going. This article is about buying and lacing – and after that about everything **below
the boot**: the holder, the steel and the curves that are ground into it. How to keep the
edges sharp is covered in the article on <a href={href('/en/sharpening')}>sharpening</a>.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
Buy the skate that is **comfortable on the foot** – not the one the box says will make you
fast. Everything else, from the model line to profiling to a second pair of steel, rarely
solves a problem a U13 player actually has.
</Callout>

## Buying skates

Skates are the most expensive piece of equipment, and they are the only one where a bad
choice holds a child back straight away. A boot that pinches ruins every practice.

### Comfort beats marketing

The boxes promise speed, energy transfer and acceleration. For a U13 player almost none of
that matters. What counts is the **fit**: the skate that best matches your child's foot is
the right one – regardless of brand, model line and colour.

Here's how to check in the shop:

- Try them on with the socks your child wears on the ice. Thin socks, not thick woollen ones.
- Check **with the knees bent**, as in the skating stance. That pushes the foot back into
  the heel.
- In this position the **heel must sit firmly**. When walking with straight legs it may lift
  slightly, with bent knees it must not. If it still lifts, the boot is too big or the shape
  doesn't fit.
- Standing upright, the toes lightly touch the toe cap. With the knees bent, they pull back a
  little.
- Walk around the shop for ten minutes. Pressure points don't show up in thirty seconds.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Don't buy to grow into">
Skate size is usually **one to one and a half sizes below street shoe size**, but it varies
by brand – always have your child measured. More than half a size of extra room doesn't
work: the foot slides, the heel lifts, and your child learns a bad skating technique. Better
to buy new skates a season earlier.
</Callout>

### Fit: how much room is in the boot

The same size comes in several widths. This means not only the width but the **volume**:
how high the instep is and how deep the heel sits.

| | narrow, low | medium | wide, high |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bauer | Fit 1 | Fit 2 | Fit 3 |
| CCM | Tapered | Regular | Wide |

In the past, letters on the boot showed this, **D** for normal and **EE** for wide; some
models still use them today. The medium version fits most people – but not everyone, and
that's exactly what the other two are for. If a skate pinches at the front or the heel won't
stay put, it's often not the size that's wrong but the fit.

### The two model lines

The big manufacturers build the same size with two characters:

- **The agile line** – **Vapor** at Bauer, **JetSpeed** at CCM. It has more forward lean
  built in from the factory, so it puts your child further forward. That helps with starts,
  tight turns and quick changes of direction.
- **The powerful line** – **Supreme** at Bauer, **Tacks** at CCM. It puts your child in a
  neutral position over the whole blade. That gives a more stable stance and suits the long,
  powerful stride that builds up speed on the straight.

"Agile" doesn't mean slow and "powerful" doesn't mean sluggish – both lines are built for
speed, they just get there in different ways. There's more on forward lean below under
<a href="#pitch-how-far-forward-your-child-stands">pitch</a>.

### Protection matters more at this age

In U13 the first really hard shots come, and skates are in the way. Look for a **sturdy toe
cap**, a padded tongue and an intact tendon guard at the back. Very cheap entry-level models
save money exactly here – what is enough for the first hours on the ice is sometimes too
little for U13 practice.

### Buying second-hand is worth it

Feet grow fast, and a pair of skates is quickly outgrown before it's worn out. Second-hand
skates from the club swap or an equipment exchange are therefore a good thing – for the same
money you often get a much better model.

What to look at when buying second-hand:

- **The boot must still give support.** If it buckles slightly to the side or is badly worn
  in at the ankle, it's done, however good it looks.
- **How much steel is left?** Blades that have been sharpened often get lower over time. New
  steel costs extra, and then the purchase may no longer pay off.
- Check the blades for **rust and nicks**, and the holder for cracks.
- Check the **rivets and eyelets**: the shop can replace loose rivets, but it costs money.
- **Try them on.** A second-hand skate has to fit too – a free one that pinches costs more
  than a bought one that fits.

Many models can be **heated and moulded again** in the shop, so that they adapt to the new
foot instead of the old one. Ask about it when you buy second-hand.

## Lacing skates properly

In U13 the kids lace their skates **themselves**. Parents no longer help in the dressing
room. So practise at home until it works even with cold fingers and under time pressure.

There are different ways to lace skates. For us coaches, a few basic rules are what matter.

### Tight at the bottom, looser at the top

The precondition for everything else: the **heel must sit firmly in the boot**. If it lifts
while skating, the boot doesn't fit or is laced too loosely at the bottom.

Lace tightly over the forefoot and over the **instep**, the arch on top of the foot. That's
where the lacing holds the foot in the boot.

**At the top eyelets** it can be looser. Your child must be able to **bring the knee
forward**. If it's too tight at the top, the movement is held back. On top of that, the
tongue then presses into the front of the shin. This pain is called **lace bite**.

What often helps: **leave out the top eyelet** or **skip the second one from the top**. That
way the boot stays stable at the top but leaves the knee room to move forward.

<Callout type="achtung" title="Don't wrap the laces around the ankles">
Many kids wrap the long ends around the top of the boot so that it feels tighter. That
locks the ankles. But to learn to skate properly, the ankles have to stay **mobile**. So
leave out the wrapping.
</Callout>

### Secure long ends

Tuck long laces **under the hockey socks** or secure them some other way, for example with a
double knot or shorter laces. When leaning hard, say in tight turns, they must neither touch
the ice nor get under the blade. Otherwise your child falls.

### When the strength isn't there yet

- **Waxed laces** don't slip as easily. That makes the boot tight enough without your child
  having to pull too hard.
- **Lace tighteners** are small hooks you use to grab the laces and pull them tight. They
  help when the fingers aren't strong enough yet.

## The three parts: boot, holder, blade

At the bottom, a skate is made of three parts, and in the shop all three are loosely called
"skate":

- **The boot.** Everything that holds the foot. It decides the fit and – more on this later –
  also how far forward your child stands.
- **The holder** (Halter). The plastic part between boot and steel. It is riveted to the boot
  and is only replaced if it breaks.
- **The blade**, also called **the steel** or **the runner** (Kufe, Stahl). The only thing
  that touches the ice. It sits in the holder, and on most of today's models it can be taken
  out.

When this article talks about sharpening, profiling or replacing below, it always means the
blade. The boot and holder stay as they are.

## Profiling: the blade from the side

The hollow (Hohlschliff) affects the blade from **below**. Then there is **profiling**, which
affects it from the **side**: a blade is slightly curved from front to back, like a very flat
rocking chair. Because of that, the whole steel never touches the ice, only a stretch in the
middle. How long that stretch is depends on the profile.

It is given as a **radius in feet**: 7', 9', 11'. This means the circle the curve is cut
from. Here it's the other way round from the hollow – a large radius means a flatter curve
and **more** steel on the ice.

<BladeProfile variant="radius" />

The lowest point of the curve is called the **pivot point**. That's where the weight sits,
and the blade rocks forwards and backwards around it. Only a short stretch either side of it
actually touches the ice.

<BladeProfile variant="pivot" />

The drawings exaggerate the curve heavily. In reality, a junior blade with a 9-foot radius
rises only about 3 millimetres off the ice at the ends.

| Radius | Effect | For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7' | very agile, wobbly, not very steady | short blades, forwards with a tight game |
| 9' | balanced | the usual factory setting |
| 11' | steadier glide, more speed on the straight | taller players, defenders |
| 13' | maximum glide, sluggish in tight turns | almost only adults |

The shorter the blade, the stronger the effect of the same radius. That's why kids' blades
often come from the factory with a little more curve than senior blades.

### Types of profile

- **Single radius.** One curve over the whole blade. The normal case, and the factory
  setting.
- **Combined profiles (dual, quad, triple).** Several radii blend into each other: shorter
  at the front and back for agility, longer in the middle for glide. Known under brand names
  like *Quad*. More expensive and only possible with special machines.
- **Pitch.** Not a profile of its own but a shift: the contact point moves forwards or
  backwards, so your child stands more on the ball of the foot or more on the heel. Often
  changed together with the profile – and by now it deserves its own section, see below.

New skates already have a profile, usually **around 9 feet**, and 7 to 9 feet on short youth

### How long does a profile last?

A profile is ground into the steel once and then lasts a long time. A normal sharpening
removes very little material, but it changes the curve a tiny bit every time – and unevenly,
because more comes off at the front and back.

As a rule of thumb, a profile lasts **20 to 30 sharpenings**. With a U13 schedule of
sharpening every two to three weeks, that's about **one to two seasons**. It's due sooner if
a deep nick had to be ground out – that takes off a lot more steel.

By the way, new replacement steel comes with the manufacturer's factory profile, not with
whatever you had on the old blade.

### Our recommendation: leave it

For U13 and for everyone who plays for fun, we recommend **no profiling**. The reasons:

- **It costs extra.** Profiling costs 30 to 50 euros depending on the shop, three to five
  times the price of a normal sharpening.
- **Hardly any shop offers it.** It needs its own machine. In many areas you have to send the
  skates away or drive a long way.
- **Your child grows out of the skates**, often after just one season. Then the money is
  gone.
- **The technique keeps changing anyway.** What feels good today may not fit in a year.

There are reasons for it – for example if a child is noticeably wobbly on the ice, or if a
coach sees a specific skating problem that profiling can fix. Then it's a conscious
decision. Without a reason like that: clean edges and the right hollow are 95 per cent of
what counts.

## Pitch: how far forward your child stands

Many modern skates come with **forward lean** built in from the factory, so your child
naturally stands further forward in them. Some manufacturers build this into the boot
itself, with a boot that leans further forward at the top or with a raised heel. Others put
a wedge under the heel, a **shim**. On the
[CCM Tacks XR70](https://www.icewarehouse.com/CCM_Tacks_XR70/descpage-SXR7.html) that's 6
millimetres, which increases the forward lean by about 3 degrees. On the
[CCM Vizion](https://modsquadhockey.com/forums/topic/77939-ccm-vizion-skates-the-pitch/)
an equally thick wedge sits on the outside between boot and holder – CCM itself says nothing
about it; players have measured it. 6 millimetres is no longer a detail; you feel it from the
very first stride.

How much forward lean a skate has depends mainly on the model line: the agile line stands
further forward than the powerful one, so **Vapor** further forward than **Supreme**, and
**JetSpeed** further forward than **Tacks**. And the manufacturers keep turning the dial –
the current top models of both lines stand
[even further forward than their predecessors](https://sportsexcellence.com/blogs/game-assist/the-biggest-hockey-skate-tech-innovations-of-2025).

What this means for you: if your child switches lines, say from Supreme to Vapor, their
stance on the ice changes, even if size and sharpening stay the same. That takes a few
practices. If your child can't get on with it at all, a wedge on the outside can be swapped
or removed completely in the shop – that's easier than grinding away at the profile.

And if you add pitch to the front through the profile on a skate like that, you are adding
to something that's already there – your child ends up too far on their toes. Ask in the
shop what's built into the boot before you have anything changed.

## Replacement steel and swappable blades

Most skates today have a **quick-release holder**: the blade is no longer riveted into the
holder but can be taken out and swapped in one move. On very cheap models and the smallest
kids' sizes, the steel is sometimes still fixed in place.

### Not all steel is the same

**The height.** Blades come in different heights – meaning how far the steel sticks out
below the holder. Taller steel has two advantages: there's more material that can be ground
away over the years, and in very tight turns the holder gets close to the ice later. On the
other hand your child stands a few millimetres higher, which changes the balance. One thing
matters above all: **both blades must be the same height**. One taller and one normal blade
on the same pair pulls your child crooked.

**The coating.** Black or gold steel is coated, usually with a very thin, hard layer. Rust
protection is certain. The manufacturers also promise edges that stay sharp for longer –
don't expect much there: the coating sits on the sides, and that's exactly where the machine
grinds it away on the first sharpening. After that, the actual edge is bare steel like on any
other blade. Coated steel costs noticeably more, often 110 to 180 euros instead of 80 to 100
euros a pair – for U13 the money is better spent elsewhere.

**The length.** Which length fits is printed as a **number in millimetres on the holder**.
That number is what counts, not the shoe size: the same skate in the same size can have a
different holder depending on the fit, for example Fit 1 and Fit 3. So always check the
holder before you order steel.

What's new is that some manufacturers want to **get more blade onto the ice** with their
current models. The [CCM Vizion](https://us.ccmhockey.com/Skates/Age-Group/Senior/683978412985.html),
for example, has an oversized holder which, according to CCM, increases the contact area on
the ice, plus
[steel that is 4 millimetres taller](https://sportsexcellence.com/en-us/blogs/spotlight/we-were-all-wrong-about-the-ccm-vizion-skate)
than usual. The idea behind it: more edge in the ice, so more grip and more distance per
stride.

What this mainly means for you: don't assume that steel from an older model of the same
brand fits, even if the quick-release system has the same name. Always compare the numbers.

### Is a second pair worth it?

The quick-release holder makes it possible to keep a **second pair of steel** in the bag. The
advantage is mostly practical: you have both pairs sharpened at the same time and then simply
swap, instead of going to the shop in between. And if someone picks up a deep nick just
before a game, the tournament isn't over.

<Callout type="achtung" title="A second pair usually doesn't move with you">
This is exactly the problem at U13: kids grow. The next skates are often a **different
brand** – and then the steel doesn't fit at all, because every quick-release system has its
own mount. And even if it stays the same system, the replacement steel is **too short** for
the new size: a bigger skate has a longer holder and needs longer steel. So the second pair
usually stays behind with the old skate.
</Callout>

On top of that, a pair of uncoated replacement steel costs about 80 to 100 euros. Coated
steel and particularly light models, for example with carbon, cost up to about 180 euros. New
steel has to be sharpened before it's used for the first time – the first sharpening costs
around 20 euros. It comes from the factory unsharpened and with the manufacturer's profile,
not with what was on the old pair.

For a U13 player with a normal training schedule it's therefore rarely worth it. It only
makes sense if one of these conditions is met:

- The foot is **hardly growing any more**, so the skates will last several seasons.
- You have **no shop nearby** and every sharpening means a long drive.
- There's a **tournament or camp** coming up, far away from any sharpening machine.

For emergencies it doesn't have to be the most expensive steel. There is often cheaper
replacement steel, and as a spare in the bag it's perfectly good enough.

<Callout type="tipp" title="Check before you buy">
The holder shows which quick-release system is fitted and which blade length it needs, as a
number in millimetres (for example 255, 263 or 272). Often the length is also printed on the
steel itself. The number on the holder is what counts – not the shoe size and not the fit.
You need the quick-release system **and** the length – the brand alone isn't enough. The same
height as the steel that's in there now makes the change easier for your child, but it isn't a
must. And never force steel into a holder it doesn't belong in.
</Callout>

<Callout type="merke" title="Rules of thumb, not laws">
Manufacturers don't name profiles and quick-release systems consistently, and the numbers
here are based on experience. If in doubt, for U13: don't change anything that doesn't
demonstrably solve a problem. How to keep the edges in shape is covered in the article on
<a href={href('/en/sharpening')}>sharpening</a>.
</Callout>
