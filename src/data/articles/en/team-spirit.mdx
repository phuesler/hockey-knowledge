A hockey game is not a quiet afternoon. Your heart is pounding, it’s loud, the puck is
fast. Sometimes everything works, sometimes nothing does. That’s a good thing. These are
exactly the moments where you learn the most, about hockey and about yourself.

<Callout type="merke" title="The short version">
- Nerves, anger and disappointment are normal. They are part of the game.
- Everyone makes mistakes. Let it go, play on, look at it later.
- Talk to yourself like you would to a good friend.
- Compare yourself with you from last month, not with the others.
- A teammate’s mistake: help, not blame. A goal against: skate to your goalie.
</Callout>

## Hockey is a game of feelings

Before the game your stomach tingles. After a mistake you get angry. After a defeat you
are sad. That doesn’t mean something is wrong with you. It means the game matters to you.

A racing heart before the game is **energy**. Your body is getting ready. You don’t have
to make the feeling go away. You learn to play with it.

What helps: **Say what you feel.** “I’m angry right now.” It sounds simple, but it works.
<a href="https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-sanlab/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2016/03/Lieberman_2007_PsychScience.pdf">Researchers have measured</a>
that a feeling gets weaker when you give it a name.

And: **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Two short breaths in through your nose,
then one long breath out through your mouth. That
<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/">calms the body</a>, on the bench
too.

## Mistakes are part of it

Everyone makes mistakes, even the pros in the NHL. A pass goes wide, you lose the puck,
you are in the wrong spot. The mistake isn’t what matters. What matters is what comes next.

**Let it go now, look at it later.** Brooding doesn’t help in the middle of a game. Your
next shift needs all of you. A small gesture helps you let go: tap your stick on the ice
once, wipe your glove across your helmet, done. What went wrong, you look at after the game
or in practice.

**A mistake shows you what to practise next.**
<a href="https://www.humanw.tu-darmstadt.de/media/orgpsy/preversioneffectivenessoferrormanagement.pdf">Studies show</a>:
people who are allowed to make mistakes while practising, and learn from them, use what
they learnt better later than people who only avoid mistakes. That’s why the
<a href={href('/en/season-concept')}>season concept</a> says: mistakes are allowed, as long as
you try something.

**The best teams talk about mistakes.** A researcher studied teams in hospitals, where
things have to happen fast under pressure. She
<a href="https://www.aamc.org/news/amy-edmondson-psychological-safety-critically-important-medicine">found something surprising</a>:
the best teams reported *more* mistakes than the others. Not because they got more wrong,
but because nobody was ashamed to say so. That was the only way they could do better next
time. The same goes for the dressing room.

## What you say to yourself

Everyone talks to themselves in their head. You can steer this **self-talk**.
<a href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/thoughts-that-win.html">Many studies show</a>:
people who tell themselves the right sentences play better. It works best with practice.

- **Short instructions** help with technique: “Head up.” “Stick on the ice.” “Soft hands.”
- **Pep talk** helps when it gets tough: “Come on, one more sprint.” “I can do this.”
- **“Not yet” instead of “not”**: “I can’t do that **yet**.” This idea is also part of the
  DEB’s <a href={href('/en/foundation-training')}>foundation training</a>.

**Talk to yourself like a friend.** Would you tell a teammate “You’re so bad”? No. So
don’t say it to yourself either. A trick: talk to yourself using your own name. “Pucky,
breathe. Next shift.” (With your own name instead of Pucky, of course.)
<a href="https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/archived-news/2014/02/talking-in-the-3rd-person-lowers-anxiety--study.html">Researchers have measured</a>
that this calms you down more than saying “I”.

**Red head, blue head.** New Zealand’s national rugby team, the All Blacks,
<a href="https://trainingground.guru/articles/how-the-all-blacks-perform-under-pressure">works with a picture</a>:
a “red head” is hectic and tense. A “blue head” is calm and focused on the task. To get from
red to blue, every player has their own small movement, for example stamping their feet
firmly or looking at a point far away. Find your own: tap your stick on the ice, look at the
clock in the rink, breathe out long.

## Your path, not everyone else’s

“Comparison is the thief of joy,” goes a well-known saying. It isn’t quite true. If you see
how a teammate shields the puck, you can copy it. Ask her how she does it. That’s a good
comparison.

A bad comparison is: “The others score more goals, so I’m bad.” That is almost never true, for two
reasons:

- **Everyone grows at a different time.** Two children of the same age can be years apart
  physically (more on this in <a href={href('/en/foundation-training')}>foundation training</a>).
  Whoever is smaller now often catches up later.
- **Everyone has different strengths.** One shoots well, another reads the game, a third
  wins every faceoff.

**Compare yourself with yourself.** What can you do today that you couldn’t do a month ago?
Backward crossovers? Carrying the puck with your head up? After practice, write down one
sentence: “Better today: …” After a few weeks you’ll see how far you’ve come.
<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02081/full">Research shows</a>:
people who measure themselves by their own progress enjoy their sport more.

## When frustration builds

Sometimes everything goes wrong. The other team is better, the referee calls everything
against you, a teammate never passes you the puck. Then you start to boil.

- **Don’t take it out on your stick or the boards.** If you go out on the ice angry, you
  make the next mistake.
- **Breathe first, then talk.** On the bench: breathe out long, say what you feel, have a
  drink.
- **“We” instead of “you”.** “We need to get back faster” helps. “You’re never back!”
  doesn’t.
- **A call, not a speech.** On the ice, the short call is what counts: “Support!”, “Open!”
  (more in the article on <a href={href('/en/support')}>support</a>).
- **Big things wait until later.** What really bothers you, you say calmly after the game,
  to your teammate or the coach.

And if a teammate says something sharp in anger: you can let that go too. The rink is loud,
and in the heat of the moment words often come out harsher than they are meant.

## When a teammate makes a mistake

They already know. Nobody loses the puck on purpose. What they need now isn’t a comment,
but a sign: we’re a team.

| This helps | This doesn’t help |
|---|---|
| Tap your stick on the ice: “Next one!” | Rolling your eyes, throwing your arms up |
| A fist bump on the bench | Silence and looking away |
| “Happens. We’ll get it back.” | “Because of you we let in a goal.” |
| Later: “Want to practise that together?” | Explaining in front of everyone what went wrong |

A pat or a fist bump says more than lots of words. In the NBA, researchers
<a href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/how-high-fives-boost-team-performance.html">counted high fives and fist bumps</a>.
Teams that touched more also played together better.

## Your goalie after a goal against

When a goal goes in, it often looks as if the goalie failed. Almost always there were
several mistakes before that, often by the skaters. Goals are scored, not let in.

So: **skate to your goalie.** A quick tap on the pads, a fist bump, “The next one’s yours.”
No blame, no head-shaking. The goalie has their own routine for letting the goal go (see
<a href={href('/en/goalies')}>goalies</a>). Your tap is part of it.

And when they stop a hard shot: skate over then too. Goalies rarely hear that they played
well.

## A good teammate off the ice

Team spirit doesn’t start at the faceoff. It starts in the dressing room.

- **Be happy about other people’s goals** as much as your own.
- **Bring new players in.** Nobody new sits alone. Show them where things are.
- **Lend a hand**: carrying, setting up the goals, collecting pucks.
- **The group chat has the same rules as the dressing room.** No jokes about mistakes,
  nobody is left out.
- **Say thank you**: for the pass, for the help at the back, for the lift to the game.

<Callout type="tipp" title="For parents">
On the drive home, heads are still full. Give your child time. Instead of analysing the
game, one sentence is often enough: “I love watching you play.” Ask later: What was fun?
What did you learn today? Praise effort and courage, not just goals. Don’t speak badly
about teammates, the goalie or the coaches in front of your child. And don’t compare your
child with others. They grow at their own pace.
</Callout>

<Callout type="merke" title="Tips, not magic spells">
The sentences and tricks in this article are rules of thumb. Not every one helps everyone.
Try out what suits you. The studies behind them are linked. Many were done with adults.
Their results are guideline values, not a guarantee.
</Callout>
